## INTRODUCTION

The question is not whether a generator company must drive through a closed road. Harborlight accepts the agreement's prohibition on unlawful or unreasonably dangerous transport. The question is whether the same agreement's reasonable-efforts undertaking has any enforceable work to do after an initial road obstruction occurs. The Superior Court answered no: it characterized that sentence as an operational aspiration and treated later disputes about resource and route inquiry as immaterial. [R-013 §II]. That interpretation supported a judgment disposing of the entire complaint without deciding the factual dispute about reasonable efforts.

Harborlight seeks vacatur and remand, not a finding that its inventory claim has been proved. The written record leaves genuine questions about breach, practicability and loss. Recognizing those questions is different from resolving them for Harborlight. The requested relief would require the trial court to address the contractual undertaking the parties actually wrote and then determine what remains to be tried.

## FACTS AND PROCEDURAL HISTORY

The parties executed their agreement on May 17, 2024. Harborlight purchased standby planning and a mobile-generation response for a refrigerated facility near Ellsworth, with a listed requirement of at least 180 kW. The retainer was $45,600 annually, paid in monthly installments of $3,800. It did not purchase a permanent on-site generator or reserve a particular serial-numbered unit exclusively to Harborlight. [R-003 §§1–2, Schedule A; R-005 §§1–4].

Under section 3.1, a qualifying notice triggers a twelve-hour hookup undertaking, subject to section 3.2. Section 3.2 addresses external delay and then requires commercially reasonable efforts to minimize that delay, including consideration of alternate equipment or transport routes when practicable. Section 3.3 addresses unlawful transport and unreasonable danger. [R-003 §§3.1–3.3]. The relationship among those provisions is the contractual question presented.

Utility power failed at 05:40 on November 14, 2024. Frost gave qualifying notice at 09:55. The submitted log records a routing review at 10:20 and a mobilization pause at 10:44 following a closure report. Harborlight does not contend that Provider caused the grid failure or that the initial closure was invented. [R-004 §§3–6; R-006 §§2–3; R-007].

Later events matter to the claim. At 14:10 the log identifies a 200-kW Belfast unit as shop-ready, subject to provisional reservation B-17. A road bulletin conditionally permitted commercial detour B after 16:15 and required individual checks. Provider recorded the information at 16:21. Neither the bulletin nor the resource notation alone certifies a lawful trip for the actual rig. [R-006 §§4–7; R-007; R-008 §C].

Frost sought an update at approximately 17:05. The log records customer contact at 17:08. Her email followed at 17:12, and the Provider's written response at 18:02 deferred resource review to the following day. These are separate sources and recorded times. The submitted extract next records route checking at 15:55 on November 15 and dispatch at 16:45. Energization occurred at 07:20 on November 16. [R-004 §§7–12; R-006 §§9–10; R-007; R-008 §§A–B].

Sutherland believed labor and escort resources would be difficult to obtain. He did not personally confirm the alternate route after 16:15 until the next afternoon. His affidavit does not attach a carrier refusal or a driver's sworn statement. Harborlight does not contend that his affidavit admits a feasible delivery; it contends that the described conduct does not conclusively establish commercially reasonable efforts. [R-006 §§6–8,12–13].

Harborlight filed its complaint on August 8, 2025. Provider moved for summary judgment on February 12, 2026. Harborlight opposed the motion on March 5, with its own responses and additional facts; Provider responded on March 16. The motion was submitted on papers without an evidentiary hearing. The Superior Court entered judgment on June 3 under its interpretation of the contract and expressly did not decide causation as an independent ground. Harborlight filed notice of appeal on June 16. [R-001; R-010; R-011; R-012; R-013; R-017].

## ISSUES PRESENTED

1. Whether the Superior Court correctly construed the reasonable-efforts sentence in section 3.2 as an operational aspiration that could not support liability for a severe-weather delay.

2. Whether, under a construction giving that sentence enforceable effect, the record permits judgment for Provider despite the disputes concerning resource inquiry, route verification and timing.

3. Whether unresolved proof of loss supports the existing judgment when the trial court expressly declined to decide that issue as a separate ground.

## ARGUMENT I. THE TEXT DISTINGUISHES AN EXTERNAL OBSTACLE FROM THE RESPONSE TO IT

### A. Standard and the narrow use of the cited cases

Interpretation and the existence of contractual ambiguity present legal questions reviewed de novo. Keep, paragraphs 21–22; Dahlem, paragraph 22. These decisions provide an interpretive framework. Harborlight does not represent either as a holding about generator dispatch, transport safety or this specific reasonable-efforts clause.

### B. Both sentences must remain part of the inquiry

The first sentence of section 3.2 concerns delay caused by conditions beyond Provider's reasonable control. The second concerns efforts to minimize such a delay. An agreement can protect a party from consequences it cannot avoid while requiring reasonable action within the circumstances it faces. That reading does not turn a reasonable-efforts undertaking into an unconditional result guarantee.

The alternative reading adopted below gives the initial storm an effect that the writing does not expressly assign to it. Once severe weather causes an initial obstruction, the court's construction makes later effort irrelevant to damages regardless of what the Provider could reasonably have investigated or done. Yet the contract specifically identifies alternate resources and routes. Those examples describe a response to an obstruction, not a promise that no obstruction will occur.

The distinction also explains section 3.3. An obligation to consider practicable alternatives does not require a prohibited trip. The safety clause limits permissible action; it does not say that learning whether action is permissible is always optional. The parties can dispute whether a reasonable inquiry was possible without disputing the validity of the closure order.

### C. The retainer does not answer the textual question

Provider emphasizes that Harborlight did not purchase insurance. Harborlight agrees that the retainer did not guarantee uninterrupted utility service. The complaint seeks to enforce a particular undertaking concerning efforts after notice. Payment of a retainer does not prove breach, but the absence of an insurance policy does not eliminate every contractual duty that may affect the extent of a loss.

Nor does the lack of an exclusively dedicated generator decide the issue. The claim does not depend on ownership of the Belfast unit. It concerns commercially reasonable consideration of available alternatives, subject to other customers' legitimate commitments. The provisional reservation may support Provider's position; it does not rewrite the agreement into an automatic priority rule that the submitted writing does not contain.

### D. At minimum, the competing construction required more than the ruling below

Harborlight maintains its reading gives distinct effect to the baseline, excuse, continuing efforts and safety restriction. If the Court concludes both constructions are reasonably available, that conclusion still defeats a judgment resting solely on Provider's asserted unambiguous exclusion. Harborlight does not ask the Law Court to resolve disputed negotiations or operational custom from new evidence. It asks that the trial court's categorical reading be set aside.

## ARGUMENT II. THE SUBMITTED MATERIAL SUPPORTS A DISPUTE ABOUT EFFORTS, NOT A FINDING OF CERTAIN DELIVERY

### A. Standard

The appeal from summary judgment is reviewed de novo under M.R. Civ. P. 56(c). The parties presented their numbered positions under Rule 56(h). Dahlem, paragraph 16, addresses the review of summary judgment; its stipulated record is not a reason to treat this case's genuinely contested inferences as stipulated.

### B. Provider's answer to an outcome claim does not dispose of an efforts claim

Harborlight has not shown that a 200-kW machine could necessarily arrive that evening. It has identified a record from which the timing and extent of inquiry may be evaluated: the resource notation, conditional bulletin, deferred-review message and next-afternoon verification. [R-007; R-008]. The agreement expressly addresses efforts, not merely the final energization time. A gap in proof of certain delivery is therefore not automatically a gap in every possible proof of inadequate effort.

The Provider's own witness separates his beliefs from his personal confirmation. That candor does not establish breach, but it matters to the motion. Sutherland does not identify a particular carrier contacted and refusing a trip during the evening interval. His affidavit cannot fairly be converted into testimony about every supplier or every employee activity. [R-006 §§6–8,12]. Whether his actual assessment and response met the undertaking remains a question that the lower court's legal interpretation prevented it from addressing.

### C. Harborlight has preserved the record's limits

The response to the supporting facts does not deny the existence of the storm or the initial pause. It qualifies the resource and route propositions by reference to the actual documents. [R-011 §§B–C]. The additional-facts statement acknowledges that an extract is not a complete history and that a bulletin is not a rig-specific certificate. Provider's responses admit the entries while contesting the requested inferences. Those are the positions the Law Court should review, not an exaggerated version in which Harborlight claims the bulletin proves a delivery schedule.

The Court need not decide that a jury would accept Harborlight's inference. It need only determine whether the judgment can stand on a construction that makes the dispute immaterial without evaluating the continuing-efforts undertaking. Remand permits the trial court to address that question under the correct construction.

## ARGUMENT III. REMAND DOES NOT AWARD THE CLAIMED LOSS

The loss summary is limited. Frost cannot identify the precise irreversible-spoilage hour for every item. The $91,400 is book value, and some of the $12,000 response expense could have arisen anyway. [R-004 §§13–16; R-009]. Those admissions remain part of the record if judgment is vacated.

The Superior Court noted substantial causation questions but expressly did not decide them as an independent ground. [R-013 §III]. Harborlight objects to treating that observation as a factual determination of no avoided loss. It is not seeking to evade its proof burden. It seeks the opportunity to meet the relevant issues under the undertaking the contract actually preserves.

If the Law Court considers an alternative legal ground, Harborlight requests that it distinguish the insufficiency of a final damages demand from the elimination of every possible compensable loss on this motion record. A remand may leave substantial obstacles for Harborlight. The existence of those obstacles does not make the trial court's contractual interpretation correct.

## CONCLUSION

Vacate the June 3 judgment and remand for further proceedings under a construction that gives effect to the reasonable-efforts language, or for proceedings addressing any interpretive ambiguity the Court identifies. Do not enter liability or damages findings for Harborlight. Leave properly preserved factual, causal and remedial disputes for the proceedings to which they belong.
