DEPOSITION OF CELESTE A. VENN — January 23, 2026
Counsel Orell and Halwick appearing; Gavin M. Lorne reporting.
Q (Orell). How did you know Ms. Sedgewick during the period in question?
A. We were representatives on the same customer support team. We worked in the same general office area. We handled different cases but had the same basic responsibilities.
Q. Were you her supervisor?
A. No. I did not approve her punches, corrections or payroll.
Q. Did you have access to her payroll record?
A. No. I could see my own entries but not hers. I cannot testify that a particular minute she worked was paid or unpaid.

<!-- PAGE 2 -->
Q. Explain what happened when a customer case was close to completion near the end of a shift.
A. The representative would try to leave a sufficient note and the appropriate status. If more work was required, a handoff could be arranged. A lead could help decide where the case should go. Sometimes finishing a note was the simplest way to avoid leaving unclear information for the next person.
Q. Was writing the note part of your job?
A. Yes. It was not something we did for personal reasons. It told the next representative what had happened and what remained.
Q. Was that also true for Ms. Sedgewick?
A. She had the same general responsibility. I did not review all of her cases.

<!-- PAGE 3 -->
Q. What were the team's ordinary hours?
A. The shift I worked with her usually ended at five. I remember that because we would prepare to leave around then.
Q. Did everyone leave at exactly five?
A. No. There could be a customer still on the line, a handoff, a conversation or other activity. Being in the office after five did not necessarily mean that someone was doing paid or unpaid work.
Q. Could you see the timekeeping screen on Ms. Sedgewick's computer?
A. Not as a routine matter. I was at my own workstation. I did not monitor her punch times.

<!-- PAGE 4 -->
Q. Did you see her working on case notes after the scheduled end on any occasions?
A. I recall occasions when she was still at her station dealing with case information while I was getting ready to leave. I cannot give you dates for those occasions. I did not look at her time record.
Q. What leads you to describe it as case information rather than a personal message?
A. I remember the general activity of closing cases in our workspace, including her referring to notes. I cannot identify the text or case numbers. I would not claim I read every item on her screen.
Q. Can you testify that she had already punched out?
A. No, not from my own observation of a punch.

<!-- PAGE 5 -->
Q. Was it common to discuss finishing cases before leaving?
A. Yes. There were reminders about documentation and about arranging coverage if something was unfinished. We understood that a proper record mattered. Nobody wanted a case returned because the note was inadequate.
Q. Did you receive the May 13 team reminder?
A. I recognize the reminder in the exhibit. It referred to finishing assigned notes and also addressed notifying a lead if the work could not be completed.
Q. Did the message say to work without pay?
A. No. I do not read it as saying that. It contemplated additional paid time or a handoff.

<!-- PAGE 6 -->
Q. Did the team feel pressure to finish notes?
A. There was ordinary pressure to keep the queue moving and leave usable records. I can describe how the work felt to me. I cannot tell you that every employee understood the reminders in the same way.
Q. Did you fear that a case would be returned if a note were incomplete?
A. I wanted to avoid that. An incomplete record could make more work for the next shift and might be discussed with a lead.
Q. Did you ever receive a direction to clock out before completing a required note?
A. Not that I can recall. I remember the importance of finishing, but not an express instruction to omit time.

<!-- PAGE 7 -->
Q. Was advance approval required to stay longer than the expected shift?
A. The practice was to tell a lead or supervisor and arrange it. They wanted to manage staffing. The written instruction was to record work time.
Q. Did you understand that a failure to arrange extra time meant the company could refuse to pay for work actually done?
A. I did not understand it that way. The scheduling question and the time record were different matters.
Q. Could it nonetheless be inconvenient to ask someone to take over a case right at the end?
A. Yes. It could require explaining the case. That does not mean that a handoff was unavailable.

<!-- PAGE 8 -->
Q. Did you ever submit a correction for your own time?
A. I knew how to ask about a correction and that payroll had a procedure. My personal payroll history is not the subject of the documents here, and I have not brought it to the deposition.
Q. Have you seen a refusal to correct Ms. Sedgewick's time?
A. No. I was not involved in her corrections.
Q. Do you know whether she reported the four dates before litigation?
A. No. I do not know what she communicated privately to her supervisor or payroll.
Q. Have you seen the May 20 private message?
A. Only in connection with the case. It was not sent to me.

<!-- PAGE 9 -->
Q. Did Ms. Sedgewick ever say in your presence that closing work sometimes extended beyond the schedule?
A. We discussed the practical problem of finishing cases around closing time. I remember general conversations, not a dated report of a particular number of unpaid minutes.
Q. Can you identify when any particular conversation occurred?
A. I cannot. It would be wrong to assign it to one of the four dates based only on the exhibit.
Q. Did the supervisor attend those conversations?
A. I do not recall him being present for a particular conversation of that kind. I cannot say what he knew as a result of something I heard.

<!-- PAGE 10 -->
Q. Was Mr. Brice sometimes present in the office near five?
A. Yes, sometimes supervisors or leads were present. I did not keep a daily attendance record for him.
Q. Could he see all employee workstations from wherever he was?
A. No. The layout and where a person stood mattered. Someone walking through the area might see a person at a desk without knowing what was on the screen or whether that person had punched out.
Q. Did you see him observe Ms. Sedgewick completing a note after a recorded out punch?
A. I cannot identify such an occasion. I did not monitor both her punch and his position.

<!-- PAGE 11 -->
Q. If a manager knew that a representative had already punched out and was doing an assigned task, would the task still be useful to the company?
A. A properly completed case note is useful regardless of the punch. Whether a manager knew the punch had happened is a different factual question.
Q. Did you know that the system preserved save events?
A. I understood that records were retained, but I did not know the details of the audit table or how timestamps were generated.
Q. Can you interpret the selected report to establish exact minutes?
A. No. I have no technical training in that system's audit services.

<!-- PAGE 12 -->
Q (Halwick). Did you personally observe the alleged work on May 2?
A. I cannot say that I did on that date.
Q. May 9?
A. I cannot identify the date of my observations.
Q. May 16?
A. Same answer.
Q. May 23?
A. I do not recall a specific event on that date.
Q. Are you saying that an employee who remained at her desk after five necessarily had punched out?
A. No. She could still be on paid time or doing something else. I cannot tell from presence at a desk.
Q. Did you review Ms. Sedgewick's pay statements?
A. No.

<!-- PAGE 13 -->
Q. Could a representative complete a note before five and remain in the office afterward?
A. Yes.
Q. Could someone remain after the schedule because of an approved extension?
A. Yes.
Q. Could a note be partially written while speaking to the customer?
A. Yes, that often happened.
Q. Does that mean every save after five necessarily involved new text written after five?
A. No. I would not say that. A save could be of something already prepared.
Q. Was saving a note and ensuring the status was correct part of the work?
A. Yes. If the person was doing those operations, they were part of the case responsibilities.

<!-- PAGE 14 -->
Q. Was there a lead who could arrange a handoff?
A. That was part of the ordinary process. There could be circumstances when a person was busy, but the process existed.
Q. Did Ms. Sedgewick tell you that a lead had specifically rejected a request for paid extra time on one of the four dates?
A. I do not recall such a statement. I did not witness a specific rejection.
Q. Did you see a manager change her punches to remove time?
A. No.
Q. Were you ever authorized to examine the payroll corrections of other representatives?
A. No. My testimony should not be understood as an audit of the company's payroll.

<!-- PAGE 15 -->
Q. The team reminder mentions paid extra time. Did you understand that a representative could notify the lead and stay clocked in?
A. Yes, if work was required and it was arranged. The reminder also contemplated reassignment.
Q. Did you understand it as directing employees to finish cases without regard to the clock?
A. It emphasized finishing properly, but I did not understand it to excuse inaccurate punches.
Q. Did you hear anyone say that a few minutes did not count for wage purposes?
A. I cannot recall a specific instruction of that kind. People sometimes spoke informally about a task taking only a moment, but that is not the same as a payroll rule.

<!-- PAGE 16 -->
Q. Have you discussed the lawsuit with Ms. Sedgewick?
A. I know about the general subject and that I was asked to testify. I am giving my own recollection. I was not asked to adopt her account of a particular date.
Q. Do you have a contemporaneous personal notebook establishing her closing times?
A. No.
Q. Did you prepare a report comparing her event data and punches?
A. No.
Q. Would you be able to distinguish eight minutes of work from a two-minute save and several minutes of unrelated activity by looking at a person across the office?
A. No. I did not continuously monitor another employee.

<!-- PAGE 17 -->
Q (Orell). Does your inability to identify particular dates mean you never saw representatives performing closing tasks after the scheduled time?
A. No. I did see that general activity. It is just not something I can assign to the exact dates here.
Q. Did you regard closing notes as optional personal activity?
A. No. They were part of our job.
Q. Could the requirement to leave a proper record make a representative choose to finish a short note rather than interrupt a lead?
A. That was a possibility. I cannot testify why Ms. Sedgewick made a particular choice. I can explain the practical work situation as I experienced it.

<!-- PAGE 18 -->
Q. Did the written reminders and the practical expectation of complete notes conflict in every instance?
A. No. A person could finish on the clock or arrange coverage. The difficulty arose when a case was nearly finished and the scheduled shift was ending.
Q. Can you determine whether Ms. Sedgewick had a real opportunity to request a correction for the dates at issue?
A. I cannot determine her specific circumstances. I know that the company had a procedure. I did not see her use it or try to use it for those particular dates.
Q. Does the fact that you did not see a request establish that she did not communicate with her supervisor?
A. No. I was not privy to their private communications.

<!-- PAGE 19 -->
Q (Halwick). Is there any specific amount of unpaid work that you can establish from your personal observations?
A. No.
Q (Orell). Is your evidence that the general closing work was part of the employer's assigned operations?
A. Yes. That is what I know from working on the same team.
Q. Have you offered any conclusion about whether the employer is legally responsible for the four alleged events?
A. No. I am not offering a legal conclusion. I have described my observations and their limits.
REPORTER: The parties reserved objections other than form. The witness was excused after completing the examination. No further testimony was taken on this date.
