DEFENDANT'S REPLY — June 8, 2026
Plaintiff correctly disclaims a calculation treating the full elapsed time as compensable. That disclaimer underscores the problem: her opposition identifies no grounded duration estimate for any of the four workweeks. Plaintiff's description of the business functions at issue is not disputed. The deficiency lies in connecting an amount of actual work to the time outside paid hours. A jury cannot award overtime for the fact that case information existed after five without determining actual work. The employer is not asking the court to prefer its witnesses' testimony to plaintiff's. It asks the court to distinguish her recollection of completing notes from speculation about the duration after the punch.

<!-- PAGE 10 -->
I. THE INITIAL SHOWING STILL MATTERS
Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co., 328 U.S. 680, 687–88 (1946) [[AUTH:AND:P1]] addresses the consequences of an employer's inadequate records after proof of uncompensated work and a reasonable inference of its amount and extent. It does not eliminate that initial showing. The selected export is not a continuous activity record; some tasks might have been completed in seconds or accepted by the server after earlier human interaction. IDEP 16–20. Plaintiff may recognize the general workflow without possessing a grounded recollection of the relevant intervals. Her testimony about the selected dates and the coworker's general observations do not supply a measure of how much additional work took place in any particular week.

<!-- PAGE 11 -->
II. THE NOTICE WAS ANSWERED, NOT IGNORED
Forrester v. Roth's I.G.A. Foodliner, Inc., 646 F.2d 413, 414–15 (9th Cir. 1981) [[AUTH:FORE:P1]] does not allow an employer to evade its obligations by closing its eyes. That is not what the record shows here. The supervisor answered the individual message the following morning, directed paid performance, asked for dates and actual time for missed work, and offered help. MSG 3. Plaintiff did not provide that information for the four disputed dates. The practical purpose of the request was to identify the work and correct the time; it was not a condition that the employee forfeit wages by failing to complete a formal claim. Plaintiff's assertion that another question or a focused technical report might have been useful does not establish that the response was legally insufficient.

<!-- PAGE 12 -->
III. CADENA IS NOT A PER SE RULE
Cadena v. Customer Connexx LLC, 107 F.4th 902 (9th Cir. 2024) (slip op. at 30–31 & n.16) [[AUTH:CAD:P1]] considered a materially disputed practice concerning boot-up and shutdown time and whether workers could obtain adjustments outside scheduled hours. The present record contains an unambiguous instruction to report and a correction for the same employee involving twelve minutes. Plaintiff does not identify an actual denial for these tasks or a rule forbidding payment. Defendant does not insist that an actual denied request is indispensable in all cases. It argues that the record as a whole lacks a reasonable basis to infer knowledge of unpaid work beyond a general possibility. Judgment should be entered for defendant.

PLAINTIFF'S OPPOSITION TO SUMMARY JUDGMENT — June 1, 2026
The record permits a reasonable inference of work and employer knowledge. Plaintiff does not demand a ruling that each elapsed minute between punch and event was worked. She asks that the trier of fact evaluate her sworn description of completing assigned notes, the four pairs of accepted application transactions following recorded punches, and her direct notice to the supervisor. The employer's theory isolates each item and treats its limitations as if no other evidence existed. The governing summary judgment inquiry requires a reasonable inference from the combined record. The single claim should proceed to determination on the merits.

<!-- PAGE 14 -->
I. THE EMPLOYEE'S WORK AND THE RECORDING DEFICIENCY
Plaintiff is a nonexempt hourly representative who was paid for forty hours in each relevant week at $22 per hour. PAY 1–5; PDEP 3. Under 29 U.S.C. § 207(a)(1) [[AUTH:STAT:P1]], compensable work beyond those forty hours requires overtime payment. Plaintiff testified that completion of case notes and status information was assigned work; she remembers finishing notes after punching out and identified the dates using the records. PDEP 7–13, 16, 22, 27, 30–31. The case-note transactions and status changes were associated with her application account, not merely sessions expiring because of inactivity. LOG 2–5; IDEP 4–10. The employee's account, unlike the server events alone, addresses the actual work.

<!-- PAGE 15 -->
II. THE PROOF DOES NOT DEPEND ON CONTINUOUS ACTIVITY
The defense properly points out that an accepted save does not measure the time of composition. Plaintiff accepts that limitation. But submitting a note and ensuring that a case is closed are themselves assigned tasks, even if text was partially prepared earlier. PDEP 12, 31; WDEP 13. The custodian found no identified automatic batch process or positive latency measurement that explains the selected post-punch entries. IDEP 7–8. The possibility of delay is a reason to examine the evidence, not to conclude that the event was unrelated to work. Under Tolan v. Cotton, 572 U.S. 650, 656–57 (2014) [[AUTH:TOL:P1]], the competing account of what the entries signify cannot be resolved against the employee if her testimony and the data permit a reasonable inference of actual work.

<!-- PAGE 16 -->
III. A REASONABLE ESTIMATE, NOT AN EXACT TIMECLOCK
Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co., 328 U.S. 680, 687–88 (1946) [[AUTH:AND:P1]] recognizes that proof of actual unpaid work and a reasonable inference of its extent may suffice where the employer's records fail to record the work. Plaintiff has four identified dates in four different weeks, not a speculative projection across an entire year. She does not count automatic session termination as work and does not ask the court to fix damages on summary judgment. PDEP 26. The save and later status change, together with her testimony, provide a factual basis for estimating some interval of work on the identified dates, subject to the employer's rebuttal. Payroll's recorded totals are not proof of every actual minute of work. HDEP 3–4. The court should not require the very precise missing record that the claim concerns.

<!-- PAGE 17 -->
IV. DIRECT NOTICE AND REASON TO INQUIRE
Plaintiff wrote on May 20 that she sometimes had minutes of notes after the clock said out. MSG 2; PDEP 18–19. Brice recognized that the words could describe work already performed. SDEP 16–17. The statement need not include a damages calculation to alert a supervisor to possible unpaid work. The company policy instructs the manager to obtain details or notify payroll when missing work time becomes known. POL 2; SDEP 5. Brice's response explained reporting options but he does not recall a specific follow-up, notifying payroll, or requesting a focused report. SDEP 18–20, 24–25. The custodian explains that the employer could request records for an account and range of dates without already knowing all case numbers. IDEP 12–15.

<!-- PAGE 18 -->
V. A FORM IS NOT THE STATUTORY STANDARD
Forrester v. Roth's I.G.A. Foodliner, Inc., 646 F.2d 413, 414–15 (9th Cir. 1981) [[AUTH:FORE:P1]] holds that an employer with actual or constructive knowledge may not stand by and permit uncompensated work simply because no claim for the compensation was made. Its affirmance concerned an employee who had not mentioned unpaid overtime and deliberately omitted it from his records despite knowing he would be paid. Here there is a direct contemporaneous message. Cadena v. Customer Connexx LLC, 107 F.4th 902 (9th Cir. 2024) (slip op. at 30–31 & n.16) [[AUTH:CAD:P1]] emphasizes that reliance on a correction mechanism must be evaluated in the context of evidence about whether employees can receive compensation in practice. The separate May 30 callback correction demonstrates that small corrections can be processed. It does not show that the closing work in the earlier weeks was investigated or paid.

<!-- PAGE 19 -->
VI. THE FOURTH DATE
The last identified event is May 23, after the employee's message and the supervisor's response. LOG 5; PDEP 22–23; SDEP 21. The factfinder could infer the supervisor reasonably believed the instruction to stay on the clock resolved the problem, as defendant argues. It could also infer that the supervisor had notice of an existing and potentially recurring work practice and should have followed up. The records do not establish actual contemporaneous observation of that individual event. Plaintiff relies on the combined notice, work expectations and availability of a focused inquiry. A finding on constructive knowledge must account for the specific circumstances before and after the exchange rather than assuming the same information was available throughout.

<!-- PAGE 20 -->
CONCLUSION
Plaintiff seeks denial of the motion, not a present damages award. The factfinder may reject her proposed inferences after hearing the evidence, but the record does not compel that result. Neither the inability to establish every minute nor the absence of a completed correction form conclusively answers the disputed questions. The direct notice and the selected event sequences distinguish this case from a claim founded solely on an employer's possession of computer records. A trial can determine whether work occurred beyond the paid forty hours, whether defendant knew or should have known, and the extent of compensable time.

DEFENDANT'S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT — May 18, 2026
Defendant moves for judgment on the sole federal overtime claim. The employment classification and regular hourly wage are not disputed. The question is whether the identified evidence permits a reasonable inference that plaintiff performed unpaid overtime of which defendant had actual or constructive knowledge. The motion relies on the timekeeping policy, payroll exhibit, selected application events and deposition testimony. It does not seek a credibility finding about whether plaintiff was sincere in recalling her closing tasks. It contends instead that the testimony and records do not furnish the required factual basis for liability.

<!-- PAGE 22 -->
I. GOVERNING LAW
29 U.S.C. § 207(a)(1) [[AUTH:STAT:P1]] requires payment at the overtime rate for covered employment beyond forty hours in the workweek. The payroll record shows forty hours paid each week. Plaintiff must connect additional compensable work to those weeks. Forrester v. Roth's I.G.A. Foodliner, Inc., 646 F.2d 413, 414–15 (9th Cir. 1981) [[AUTH:FORE:P1]] distinguishes a failure to report time unknown to the employer from overtime work the employer knew or should have known was being performed. Defendant does not assert that a written policy alone discharges the obligation to pay known work. It submits that plaintiff's evidence does not establish the factual predicate of such knowledge, and that a working correction procedure is significant in evaluating what defendant could reasonably learn. Under Tolan v. Cotton, 572 U.S. 650, 656–57 (2014) [[AUTH:TOL:P1]], the motion requires crediting plaintiff's evidence and reasonable inferences, not every possible inference.

<!-- PAGE 23 -->
II. THE RECORD OF THE FOUR WEEKS
The payroll exhibit reports forty paid hours for each week from April 29 through May 26. The disputed application events appear on May 2, May 9, May 16 and May 23. PAY 1–5; LOG 2–5. Each identifies a note save and status change after the displayed 17:00 punch. Plaintiff admits the time system did not automatically require a punch at 17:00; she used a punch control. PDEP 4. The custodian explains that accepted server events are not measurements of active human work. IDEP 3–6. The selected export does not identify when text was composed, how long the task lasted, or the amount of uninterrupted work after each punch. Defendant therefore disputes a calculation based simply on the difference between the two timestamps.

<!-- PAGE 24 -->
III. TESTIMONY DOES NOT SUPPLY A WEEKLY DURATION
Plaintiff remembers closing tasks generally and recognizes some specific dates from the records. She cannot reconstruct an exact interval on any of the dates, does not recall the text history, and does not assert continuous work from the punch to the later save. PDEP 8–13, 16, 22–23, 26. The coworker cannot identify particular dates or establish that plaintiff had punched out. WDEP 4, 12. Defendant recognizes that Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co., 328 U.S. 680, 687–88 (1946) [[AUTH:AND:P1]] does not require exact time records where the employer's records fail to capture actual work. The initial factual showing nevertheless must permit a reasonable inference of the amount and extent of uncompensated work. Defendant submits that identifying the latest recorded transaction, without a reasoned estimate of actual work time, does not make that showing.

<!-- PAGE 25 -->
IV. THE SUPERVISOR'S INFORMATION
The May 13 reminder is addressed to the team and concerns quality of notes. It expressly contemplates notice to the lead, additional paid time or a handoff. MSG 1; SDEP 12–14. It does not direct employees to punch before finishing. Plaintiff acknowledges the absence of such an instruction and cannot identify a refused request for paid additional time on a disputed date. PDEP 14–15, 21–23. The supervisor could view queue information and request historical application events, but his ordinary screen did not reconcile those events with payroll. SDEP 6–11; IDEP 11–14. Possession of records in separate departments is not identical to contemporaneous knowledge that a particular employee performed unpaid work.

<!-- PAGE 26 -->
V. THE MAY EXCHANGE AND THE CORRECTION PROCEDURE
Plaintiff's May 20 message is evidence that she raised a concern. Defendant does not deny receipt. She identified no date, case number, or duration. MSG 2; SDEP 16–17. The next morning the supervisor directed her to stay on the clock, arrange a handoff as needed, send missed time to payroll, and offered assistance. MSG 3; SDEP 18–20. There is no written reply or specific correction request for these dates. A separate twelve-minute correction for a May 30 callback was approved and processed June 4. PAY 6; HDEP 12–13. Cadena v. Customer Connexx LLC, 107 F.4th 902 (9th Cir. 2024) (slip op. at 30–31 & n.16) [[AUTH:CAD:P1]] rejected summary judgment where evidence placed in dispute whether the correction practice permitted compensation for the activities at issue. Here the record contains a specific invitation to report and an actual example of correction.

<!-- PAGE 27 -->
VI. RELIEF
Defendant's position is not that failing to fill out a form constitutes a waiver of statutory rights. Under Forrester v. Roth's I.G.A. Foodliner, Inc., 646 F.2d 413, 414–15 (9th Cir. 1981) [[AUTH:FORE:P1]], the issue is whether the employer knew or should have known about the overtime despite the reporting failure. The individual message did not identify an event that could be matched with time records, and the response offered a means to clarify the concern. No evidence establishes that defendant refused a report or received the selected technical extract during the disputed period. On this record, defendant contends the evidence of both additional work and knowledge remains insufficient to submit liability to a factfinder. Defendant requests judgment on the single claim.
