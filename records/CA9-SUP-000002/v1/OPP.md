UNITED STATES DISTRICT COURT — CENTRAL DISTRICT OF CALIFORNIA
Mira L. Sedgewick v. Harborline Support Services, LLC
SIM-CDCA-25-CV-081
PLAINTIFF'S OPPOSITION TO SUMMARY JUDGMENT — June 1, 2026
The record permits a reasonable inference of work and employer knowledge. Plaintiff does not demand a ruling that each elapsed minute between punch and event was worked. She asks that the trier of fact evaluate her sworn description of completing assigned notes, the four pairs of accepted application transactions following recorded punches, and her direct notice to the supervisor. The employer's theory isolates each item and treats its limitations as if no other evidence existed. The governing summary judgment inquiry requires a reasonable inference from the combined record. The single claim should proceed to determination on the merits.

<!-- PAGE 2 -->
I. THE EMPLOYEE'S WORK AND THE RECORDING DEFICIENCY
Plaintiff is a nonexempt hourly representative who was paid for forty hours in each relevant week at $22 per hour. PAY 1–5; PDEP 3. Under 29 U.S.C. § 207(a)(1) [[AUTH:STAT:P1]], compensable work beyond those forty hours requires overtime payment. Plaintiff testified that completion of case notes and status information was assigned work; she remembers finishing notes after punching out and identified the dates using the records. PDEP 7–13, 16, 22, 27, 30–31. The case-note transactions and status changes were associated with her application account, not merely sessions expiring because of inactivity. LOG 2–5; IDEP 4–10. The employee's account, unlike the server events alone, addresses the actual work.

<!-- PAGE 3 -->
II. THE PROOF DOES NOT DEPEND ON CONTINUOUS ACTIVITY
The defense properly points out that an accepted save does not measure the time of composition. Plaintiff accepts that limitation. But submitting a note and ensuring that a case is closed are themselves assigned tasks, even if text was partially prepared earlier. PDEP 12, 31; WDEP 13. The custodian found no identified automatic batch process or positive latency measurement that explains the selected post-punch entries. IDEP 7–8. The possibility of delay is a reason to examine the evidence, not to conclude that the event was unrelated to work. Under Tolan v. Cotton, 572 U.S. 650, 656–57 (2014) [[AUTH:TOL:P1]], the competing account of what the entries signify cannot be resolved against the employee if her testimony and the data permit a reasonable inference of actual work.

<!-- PAGE 4 -->
III. A REASONABLE ESTIMATE, NOT AN EXACT TIMECLOCK
Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co., 328 U.S. 680, 687–88 (1946) [[AUTH:AND:P1]] recognizes that proof of actual unpaid work and a reasonable inference of its extent may suffice where the employer's records fail to record the work. Plaintiff has four identified dates in four different weeks, not a speculative projection across an entire year. She does not count automatic session termination as work and does not ask the court to fix damages on summary judgment. PDEP 26. The save and later status change, together with her testimony, provide a factual basis for estimating some interval of work on the identified dates, subject to the employer's rebuttal. Payroll's recorded totals are not proof of every actual minute of work. HDEP 3–4. The court should not require the very precise missing record that the claim concerns.

<!-- PAGE 5 -->
IV. DIRECT NOTICE AND REASON TO INQUIRE
Plaintiff wrote on May 20 that she sometimes had minutes of notes after the clock said out. MSG 2; PDEP 18–19. Brice recognized that the words could describe work already performed. SDEP 16–17. The statement need not include a damages calculation to alert a supervisor to possible unpaid work. The company policy instructs the manager to obtain details or notify payroll when missing work time becomes known. POL 2; SDEP 5. Brice's response explained reporting options but he does not recall a specific follow-up, notifying payroll, or requesting a focused report. SDEP 18–20, 24–25. The custodian explains that the employer could request records for an account and range of dates without already knowing all case numbers. IDEP 12–15.

<!-- PAGE 6 -->
V. A FORM IS NOT THE STATUTORY STANDARD
Forrester v. Roth's I.G.A. Foodliner, Inc., 646 F.2d 413, 414–15 (9th Cir. 1981) [[AUTH:FORE:P1]] holds that an employer with actual or constructive knowledge may not stand by and permit uncompensated work simply because no claim for the compensation was made. Its affirmance concerned an employee who had not mentioned unpaid overtime and deliberately omitted it from his records despite knowing he would be paid. Here there is a direct contemporaneous message. Cadena v. Customer Connexx LLC, 107 F.4th 902 (9th Cir. 2024) (slip op. at 30–31 & n.16) [[AUTH:CAD:P1]] emphasizes that reliance on a correction mechanism must be evaluated in the context of evidence about whether employees can receive compensation in practice. The separate May 30 callback correction demonstrates that small corrections can be processed. It does not show that the closing work in the earlier weeks was investigated or paid.

<!-- PAGE 7 -->
VI. THE FOURTH DATE
The last identified event is May 23, after the employee's message and the supervisor's response. LOG 5; PDEP 22–23; SDEP 21. The factfinder could infer the supervisor reasonably believed the instruction to stay on the clock resolved the problem, as defendant argues. It could also infer that the supervisor had notice of an existing and potentially recurring work practice and should have followed up. The records do not establish actual contemporaneous observation of that individual event. Plaintiff relies on the combined notice, work expectations and availability of a focused inquiry. A finding on constructive knowledge must account for the specific circumstances before and after the exchange rather than assuming the same information was available throughout.

<!-- PAGE 8 -->
CONCLUSION
Plaintiff seeks denial of the motion, not a present damages award. The factfinder may reject her proposed inferences after hearing the evidence, but the record does not compel that result. Neither the inability to establish every minute nor the absence of a completed correction form conclusively answers the disputed questions. The direct notice and the selected event sequences distinguish this case from a claim founded solely on an employer's possession of computer records. A trial can determine whether work occurred beyond the paid forty hours, whether defendant knew or should have known, and the extent of compensable time.
