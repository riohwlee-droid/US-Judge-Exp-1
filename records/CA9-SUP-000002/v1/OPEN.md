ISSUES PRESENTED
Whether summary judgment was proper where the employee's testimony and business-event records identify work after recorded punches and the employer's manager received a contemporaneous message concerning that work. The obligation under 29 U.S.C. § 207(a)(1) [[AUTH:STAT:P1]] and the proof of its breach are distinct from the amount of any ultimate recovery.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE
Appellant worked as an hourly nonexempt customer support representative at $22 per hour and was paid forty hours in each of four weeks (2-ER-164; 2-ER-124). She alleged that closing notes involved uncompensated work following the recorded end of paid time. The selected record identifies note saves and status changes on May 2, 9, 16 and 23, 2024 (2-ER-159; 2-ER-160; 2-ER-161; 2-ER-162). The parties took five depositions. Appellant opposed summary judgment and argued the motion at the June 29 hearing (2-ER-21; 2-ER-11). The court granted judgment for the employer on two independent grounds: insufficient evidence of the amount and extent of work and insufficient evidence of employer knowledge (1-ER-4; 1-ER-8).

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT
The judgment treats limits of proof as if they eliminated the permissible inferences from the record. Testimony of assigned work performed after a punch is corroborated by selected business events. The employee's message and the manager's acknowledgment supply evidence concerning actual or constructive knowledge. Neither the separate correction procedure nor the inability to measure continuous activity conclusively resolves the disputed elements.
STANDARD OF REVIEW
Review is de novo. Tolan v. Cotton, 572 U.S. 650, 656–57 (2014) [[AUTH:TOL:P1]] requires drawing justifiable inferences for the nonmovant. Under Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co., 328 U.S. 680, 687–88 (1946) [[AUTH:AND:P1]], incomplete time records do not impose a requirement of precise reconstruction. Forrester v. Roth's I.G.A. Foodliner, Inc., 646 F.2d 413, 414–15 (9th Cir. 1981) [[AUTH:FORE:P1]] addresses the employer's duty when it knows or should know of overtime; appellant challenges both stated grounds for judgment.

ARGUMENT
I. The combined evidence permits a finding of actual uncompensated work.
29 U.S.C. § 207(a)(1) [[AUTH:STAT:P1]] requires overtime payment for covered employment beyond forty hours in a workweek. The paid total in each disputed week was forty. Appellant described the steps necessary to finish assigned notes and the relation of those steps to the punch system (2-ER-128; 2-ER-129; 2-ER-133). The application export shows accepted note saves followed by case-status operations, not simply an automatic session ending. The custodian distinguished the types of recorded events (2-ER-58; 2-ER-59; 2-ER-60). Appellant does not claim to know when every word was typed, but actual submission and case completion are work functions. Under Tolan v. Cotton, 572 U.S. 650, 656–57 (2014) [[AUTH:TOL:P1]], the record must be considered together rather than rejecting the inference because each individual source has limitations.

<!-- PAGE 8 -->
The district court accepted that some activity might have occurred after a punch but concluded the evidence did not permit an estimate of its extent. Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co., 328 U.S. 680, 687–88 (1946) [[AUTH:AND:P1]] requires a just and reasonable inference, not a precise clock-based measurement of every act. The employee testified about actual work on the identified dates, distinguished stronger and weaker recollections, and did not count the full period before automatic session termination (2-ER-130; 2-ER-137; 2-ER-143; 2-ER-147). The jury may determine a reasonable amount from the employee's description of short closing tasks, the timing of the business events, and rebuttal evidence. The employee does not seek to use a later server time as an irrebuttable measure of time worked. Requiring an exact alternative reconstruction at this stage risks foreclosing the very claim that time was omitted from the employer's records.

<!-- PAGE 9 -->
II. The employer received evidence of possible unpaid work.
Appellant wrote on May 20: "Sometimes I have a few minutes of notes after the clock says out." (2-ER-155). This was not a generic request that payroll explain the policy. The supervisor understood the message could describe work already performed (2-ER-109; 2-ER-110). The employer's policy calls for obtaining details or notifying payroll about missing time. Forrester v. Roth's I.G.A. Foodliner, Inc., 646 F.2d 413, 414–15 (9th Cir. 1981) [[AUTH:FORE:P1]] holds an employer with knowledge of overtime cannot simply allow the work to go uncompensated because no formal claim was submitted. Its facts concerned an employee who deliberately omitted overtime he knew would be paid and failed to mention the work. Here the contemporaneous message supplies a different evidentiary basis. The jury may infer that a reasonable supervisor would have pursued the potential discrepancy rather than regard the absence of a completed correction as dispositive.

<!-- PAGE 10 -->
The custodian testified that a focused report may be requested by account and date range without starting from an exhaustive list of case numbers (2-ER-66; 2-ER-67; 2-ER-68). That report could help identify candidate dates for a conversation. It would not measure active duration. The employer is correct that the ordinary dashboard lacks a payroll reconciliation warning. That limitation does not answer whether reasonable diligence after specific notice could have uncovered the work. The manager does not recall a separate follow-up or focused request (2-ER-112; 2-ER-113). Tolan v. Cotton, 572 U.S. 650, 656–57 (2014) [[AUTH:TOL:P1]] bars resolving reasonable competing inferences in favor of the movant. The May 23 event occurred after the exchange (2-ER-162). Even if the earlier events presented a closer knowledge question, the later one requires separate attention to the information then available.

<!-- PAGE 11 -->
III. The availability of corrections does not dispose of the claim.
The supervisor's response did not tell appellant to work without pay; it invited her to submit dates and actual time and offered help (2-ER-156). The separate callback adjustment processed June 4 was for May 30 and fell outside the disputed weeks (2-ER-169). Those facts support the employer's position that the mechanism was genuine. But Forrester v. Roth's I.G.A. Foodliner, Inc., 646 F.2d 413, 414–15 (9th Cir. 1981) [[AUTH:FORE:P1]] does not create a blanket reporting-form defense against known overtime. Cadena v. Customer Connexx LLC, 107 F.4th 902 (9th Cir. 2024) (slip op. at 30–31 & n.16) [[AUTH:CAD:P1]] illustrates the need to consider whether a correction mechanism actually permits payment for the activities in dispute; that opinion expressly reserved the separate application of Forrester. Appellant relies on the direct message here, not on a claim that Harborline categorically forbade reporting. A reasonable jury may regard the response as helpful but insufficient to address an existing recurring concern, particularly in light of the last event.

<!-- PAGE 12 -->
CONCLUSION
Appellant requests that the judgment be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings. The jury, not this Court, should determine what additional work occurred, whether the employer knew or should have known, and any amount recoverable. The appellant does not ask the Court to accept the sum of the elapsed intervals as a damages award. The initial evidentiary showing under Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co., 328 U.S. 680, 687–88 (1946) [[AUTH:AND:P1]] may be met without precise clock records, and the competing reasonable inferences regarding knowledge cannot be resolved at summary judgment.
