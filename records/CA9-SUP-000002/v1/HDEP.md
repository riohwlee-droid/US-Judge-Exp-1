DEPOSITION OF MAREN J. VOSSLER — January 16, 2026
Appearances as in prior depositions. Gavin M. Lorne, reporter.
Q (Orell). State your responsibility for hourly employees.
A. I supervise the payroll processing team. We take the recorded punches, apply the applicable pay rates, review adjustments submitted through our system, and issue pay. I am not the operational manager of the customer support representatives.
Q. Did you perform that function during April and May 2024?
A. Yes. My recollection of individual entries is largely from the payroll records produced.
Q. Can you identify the payroll exhibit?
A. Yes. It contains the time and pay information for Ms. Sedgewick for the disputed four weeks and the separate correction following that period. I reviewed it against our retained payroll records.

<!-- PAGE 2 -->
Q. Explain the distinction between a punch and a work schedule.
A. The schedule identifies when an employee is expected to work. A punch is the recorded entry that the system uses in computing paid time. They need not be identical. If someone stays longer, the employee can punch later and an adjustment may be necessary if the punch does not reflect the time actually worked.
Q. Does the payroll system automatically credit every minute after scheduled closing?
A. No. It credits the recorded punches and approved corrections. If a person finishes after the schedule, we need an accurate record of that time.
Q. Who has authority to say that the recorded time should be changed?
A. Payroll may process a documented correction. The employee's supervisor usually supplies or confirms the factual information.

<!-- PAGE 3 -->
Q. What did you find about this employee's wage classification?
A. She was treated as hourly and nonexempt. The rate for the period at issue was twenty-two dollars per hour. There is no exemption code in the record suggesting otherwise.
Q. What was the recorded paid time for each of the four weeks?
A. Forty hours in each. That is a report of recorded paid hours, not a personal observation of everything she did at work.
Q. What is the ordinary calculation if an employee worked additional compensable minutes after forty hours?
A. At that rate the overtime rate would be thirty-three dollars an hour. We would convert the additional time into the pay calculation, subject to the applicable pay rules. I cannot tell you from these entries how many additional minutes she worked.

<!-- PAGE 4 -->
Q. Turn to the first weekly portion of the exhibit. What does the end-of-shift entry on May 2 indicate?
A. An out punch of seventeen hundred hours. The record reflects a normal recorded day.
Q. Did you see the employee press the button?
A. No.
Q. Does the entry identify the last minute she performed any task?
A. Not necessarily. It identifies the punch. If a person had performed another task after the punch, this record standing alone would not reflect that.
Q. Would a pay statement showing forty hours establish that there was no unpaid work?
A. No. It establishes what we paid. Whether there was work missing from the record would require other information.

<!-- PAGE 5 -->
Q. Does payroll round a separately reported eight-minute interval down to zero merely because it is short?
A. There is no instruction to reject a correction because it is only several minutes. The relevant question in our adjustment process is whether the correction describes compensable time and whether it is accurate.
Q. Is a representative expected to obtain permission to receive pay for work already performed?
A. Our written procedure distinguishes approval of overtime in advance from reporting work that has already occurred. The supervisor may address scheduling separately, but the payroll correction is not supposed to be denied simply because work was not preapproved.
Q. Have you reviewed any disciplinary record concerning Ms. Sedgewick?
A. No. I have no basis to offer testimony about discipline in her case.

<!-- PAGE 6 -->
Q. If a supervisor contacts payroll without an employee's formal correction submission, how do you respond?
A. We ask for the date, the time, and the reason the original entry is believed wrong. We may ask for further detail or direct the supervisor to confer with the employee. The purpose is to create an adjustment that can be audited.
Q. Is the formal employee submission the only possible way for payroll to learn about missing time?
A. No. A supervisor or another appropriate source can bring a discrepancy to our attention. We still need enough reliable information to process it.
Q. Do you insist on proof of every keystroke the employee made?
A. No. That would be impractical and it is not how ordinary corrections are processed.

<!-- PAGE 7 -->
Q. Could an employee state that she worked after her punch but cannot remember precisely how long?
A. Yes. That is a possible report. We would normally try to identify the day and what happened, then obtain a reasonable account from the employee and manager. I would not regard the absence of an exact minute as necessarily the end of the inquiry.
Q. Does payroll independently decide from a vague complaint how many minutes to add?
A. No. We should not invent time. We would seek information that helps resolve the discrepancy.
Q. Are you describing an actual investigation undertaken in this case?
A. No. I am describing the procedure. I did not personally conduct a contemporaneous investigation of the four closing events.

<!-- PAGE 8 -->
Q. At the time, what information did payroll receive from the application used for customer case notes?
A. Not the detailed customer audit report marked as the separate log exhibit. We receive the time records. The ordinary payroll screen does not display case-note saves or case closures.
Q. Could the employer, as an organization, request technical records from a different department?
A. I assume so, but I cannot testify about their permissions or exact retention practices. That is a subject for the technical custodian.
Q. Did you personally compare case-note entries to the out punches each week in 2024?
A. No. That was not part of the routine payroll calculation for this group.

<!-- PAGE 9 -->
Q. Who could raise a discrepancy if a manager learned that a representative worked after a punch?
A. The manager could contact payroll or ask the representative to report it. Our policy provides for the manager to obtain information concerning missing work time and to notify payroll as appropriate.
Q. Is there an internal reason not to rely entirely on employees for accuracy?
A. Employees can make mistakes, including forgetting to punch or punching too early. Supervisors may also discover an error. The system depends on employees reporting accurately and supervisors bringing known errors to us.
Q. Did you ever advise this supervisor to ignore a report of unpaid time?
A. No. I have no recollection or document showing that I gave any such instruction.

<!-- PAGE 10 -->
Q. Did you receive the May 20 message from Ms. Sedgewick when it was sent?
A. No, not that I recall. I have seen the exhibit in connection with this litigation. I was not a participant in that exchange.
Q. Did Mr. Brice call you on May 21 to say there might be missing time for the prior weeks?
A. I do not recall such a call. I found no correction for those four closing events in the payroll material.
Q. Can you say there was no informal communication anywhere in the company?
A. No. My testimony is limited to my recollection and our records. Absence of a correction in payroll does not prove that nobody in another department heard about the problem.

<!-- PAGE 11 -->
Q. Would the words 'Sometimes I have a few minutes of notes after the clock says out' give you an exact amount to pay?
A. No. They would raise a question about possible missing time. I would want the employee or manager to identify dates and the circumstances.
Q. Would you interpret the statement as necessarily describing something that might happen only in the future?
A. I would not assume that. The wording could describe an actual experience. I was not the person who received it, and I cannot supply the manager's interpretation.
Q. If payroll received that message directly, could it ask for more information?
A. Yes. That is different from saying we could know the amount immediately.

<!-- PAGE 12 -->
Q. Please identify the separate adjustment shown after the four-week period.
A. It relates to a callback on May 30, 2024, with twelve minutes entered as an adjustment processed on June 4.
Q. What does it tell you about the correction process?
A. An adjustment was made. The record shows a mechanism was used and pay was processed for that time.
Q. Does it correspond to a note save on May 2, May 9, May 16, or May 23?
A. No. It is for a different date and a different reported activity.
Q. Can you apply the twelve-minute amount to the four earlier weeks?
A. No. We cannot move minutes from one workweek to another as if all time were interchangeable.

<!-- PAGE 13 -->
Q. Was the twelve-minute adjustment a large monetary item?
A. It was a small amount of time. Payroll processes adjustments that are small as well as those that are larger.
Q. Did the adjustment require the employee to provide a complete export of customer records?
A. Not as reflected in the payroll record. We had a reported date, time and reason sufficient for processing.
Q. Does the document tell you every conversation about the adjustment?
A. No. It identifies the correction and when it was processed. It is not a transcript of any discussion.
Q. Is there any notation that a correction for the four disputed dates was rejected?
A. No rejection of those dates appears in the produced payroll records.

<!-- PAGE 14 -->
Q (Halwick). Did the company have a working procedure for bringing missing time to payroll?
A. Yes. The procedure was available and in actual use. The separate adjustment is one instance involving this employee.
Q. Are you aware of a complaint by her to payroll during the four disputed weeks?
A. Not from my recollection or the records I checked.
Q. Do the payroll records show that she provided actual dates and time amounts for the disputed closing events before pay was issued?
A. No. I found no such correction entry.
Q. Can payroll pay a particular time amount without receiving a sufficiently specific factual basis?
A. It cannot accurately calculate an amount in that circumstance. More information would have to be obtained.

<!-- PAGE 15 -->
Q. What would happen if an employee worked while clocked out, did not report it, and no supervisor or payroll employee learned of it?
A. The system would calculate pay from the punches. I cannot tell you that such a calculation would necessarily reflect all actual work. I can tell you that the system would have no additional time entry to process.
Q. Could payroll infer work merely from a scheduled shift?
A. No. The schedule does not identify unrecorded tasks performed outside of it.
Q. Did the retained payroll record disclose a specific discrepancy on any of these four closing dates?
A. Not by itself. The punch appears regular. The separate application record would be necessary to identify a possible discrepancy.

<!-- PAGE 16 -->
Q. If a representative was finishing a case at seventeen hundred, would an approved handoff be recorded as overtime automatically?
A. No. Payroll does not assign cases or record handoffs. Whether the employee remained on the clock would determine the time entry, absent a correction.
Q. Could a representative simply remain clocked in until work was completed?
A. The timekeeping procedure tells employees to record their actual work time. I cannot speak to whether a particular operational manager had a practical staffing difficulty.
Q. Did the company give payroll a rule requiring punches at exactly seventeen hundred even when work was continuing?
A. No. I have seen no such rule. The schedule and actual work time are distinct.

<!-- PAGE 17 -->
Q (Orell). You were asked about the available reporting procedure. Does the existence of that procedure establish that a manager in another department had no knowledge of missing time?
A. No. I cannot determine a manager's knowledge from the payroll procedure.
Q. If the employee did not give an exact duration but the manager had other relevant information, could that information be brought to payroll?
A. Yes.
Q. Is the purpose of an adjustment to reward an employee for correctly filling in a form?
A. No. The purpose is to correct paid time. The form is a means of documenting the correction.
Q. Did payroll request the case audit records after the May message?
A. Not to my recollection. I did not receive the message at that time.

<!-- PAGE 18 -->
Q. If the four weekly totals were accurate as recorded but one additional compensable minute occurred each week, would the recorded amount differ from actual work?
A. Yes, in your hypothetical. Whether that happened is not something I can answer from the payroll records.
Q. Does the $22 hourly rate mean that the employee would be paid at straight time for all additional minutes?
A. Not if they are compensable overtime over forty hours in the workweek. The overtime calculation ordinarily uses time and a half.
Q. Are you offering a calculation of damages here?
A. No. I would need an established amount of additional work in each week and any other applicable payroll information.

<!-- PAGE 19 -->
Q (Halwick). Have you certified that the four case-note events are compensable work?
A. No.
Q. Have you certified that no such work occurred?
A. No. My certification concerns the retained payroll records and the adjustment entry.
Q (Orell). Did you find any entry paying the closing-time work alleged for the four disputed dates?
A. No separate adjustment for those events appears.
Q. Is that different from an entry showing that the employee never performed any such work?
A. Yes. They are different propositions.
