DEFENDANT'S REPLY — June 8, 2026
Plaintiff correctly disclaims a calculation treating the full elapsed time as compensable. That disclaimer underscores the problem: her opposition identifies no grounded duration estimate for any of the four workweeks. Plaintiff's description of the business functions at issue is not disputed. The deficiency lies in connecting an amount of actual work to the time outside paid hours. A jury cannot award overtime for the fact that case information existed after five without determining actual work. The employer is not asking the court to prefer its witnesses' testimony to plaintiff's. It asks the court to distinguish her recollection of completing notes from speculation about the duration after the punch.

I. THE INITIAL SHOWING STILL MATTERS
Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co., 328 U.S. 680, 687–88 (1946) [[AUTH:AND:P1]] addresses the consequences of an employer's inadequate records after proof of uncompensated work and a reasonable inference of its amount and extent. It does not eliminate that initial showing. The selected export is not a continuous activity record; some tasks might have been completed in seconds or accepted by the server after earlier human interaction. IDEP 16–20. Plaintiff may recognize the general workflow without possessing a grounded recollection of the relevant intervals. Her testimony about the selected dates and the coworker's general observations do not supply a measure of how much additional work took place in any particular week.

II. THE NOTICE WAS ANSWERED, NOT IGNORED
Forrester v. Roth's I.G.A. Foodliner, Inc., 646 F.2d 413, 414–15 (9th Cir. 1981) [[AUTH:FORE:P1]] does not allow an employer to evade its obligations by closing its eyes. That is not what the record shows here. The supervisor answered the individual message the following morning, directed paid performance, asked for dates and actual time for missed work, and offered help. MSG 3. Plaintiff did not provide that information for the four disputed dates. The practical purpose of the request was to identify the work and correct the time; it was not a condition that the employee forfeit wages by failing to complete a formal claim. Plaintiff's assertion that another question or a focused technical report might have been useful does not establish that the response was legally insufficient.

<!-- PAGE 4 -->
III. CADENA IS NOT A PER SE RULE
Cadena v. Customer Connexx LLC, 107 F.4th 902 (9th Cir. 2024) (slip op. at 30–31 & n.16) [[AUTH:CAD:P1]] considered a materially disputed practice concerning boot-up and shutdown time and whether workers could obtain adjustments outside scheduled hours. The present record contains an unambiguous instruction to report and a correction for the same employee involving twelve minutes. Plaintiff does not identify an actual denial for these tasks or a rule forbidding payment. Defendant does not insist that an actual denied request is indispensable in all cases. It argues that the record as a whole lacks a reasonable basis to infer knowledge of unpaid work beyond a general possibility. Judgment should be entered for defendant.
