UNITED STATES DISTRICT COURT — CENTRAL DISTRICT OF CALIFORNIA, WESTERN DIVISION
Mira L. Sedgewick v. Harborline Support Services, LLC
SIM-CDCA-25-CV-081
DEFENDANT'S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT — May 18, 2026
Defendant moves for judgment on the sole federal overtime claim. The employment classification and regular hourly wage are not disputed. The question is whether the identified evidence permits a reasonable inference that plaintiff performed unpaid overtime of which defendant had actual or constructive knowledge. The motion relies on the timekeeping policy, payroll exhibit, selected application events and deposition testimony. It does not seek a credibility finding about whether plaintiff was sincere in recalling her closing tasks. It contends instead that the testimony and records do not furnish the required factual basis for liability.

<!-- PAGE 2 -->
I. GOVERNING LAW
29 U.S.C. § 207(a)(1) [[AUTH:STAT:P1]] requires payment at the overtime rate for covered employment beyond forty hours in the workweek. The payroll record shows forty hours paid each week. Plaintiff must connect additional compensable work to those weeks. Forrester v. Roth's I.G.A. Foodliner, Inc., 646 F.2d 413, 414–15 (9th Cir. 1981) [[AUTH:FORE:P1]] distinguishes a failure to report time unknown to the employer from overtime work the employer knew or should have known was being performed. Defendant does not assert that a written policy alone discharges the obligation to pay known work. It submits that plaintiff's evidence does not establish the factual predicate of such knowledge, and that a working correction procedure is significant in evaluating what defendant could reasonably learn. Under Tolan v. Cotton, 572 U.S. 650, 656–57 (2014) [[AUTH:TOL:P1]], the motion requires crediting plaintiff's evidence and reasonable inferences, not every possible inference.

II. THE RECORD OF THE FOUR WEEKS
The payroll exhibit reports forty paid hours for each week from April 29 through May 26. The disputed application events appear on May 2, May 9, May 16 and May 23. PAY 1–5; LOG 2–5. Each identifies a note save and status change after the displayed 17:00 punch. Plaintiff admits the time system did not automatically require a punch at 17:00; she used a punch control. PDEP 4. The custodian explains that accepted server events are not measurements of active human work. IDEP 3–6. The selected export does not identify when text was composed, how long the task lasted, or the amount of uninterrupted work after each punch. Defendant therefore disputes a calculation based simply on the difference between the two timestamps.

<!-- PAGE 4 -->
III. TESTIMONY DOES NOT SUPPLY A WEEKLY DURATION
Plaintiff remembers closing tasks generally and recognizes some specific dates from the records. She cannot reconstruct an exact interval on any of the dates, does not recall the text history, and does not assert continuous work from the punch to the later save. PDEP 8–13, 16, 22–23, 26. The coworker cannot identify particular dates or establish that plaintiff had punched out. WDEP 4, 12. Defendant recognizes that Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co., 328 U.S. 680, 687–88 (1946) [[AUTH:AND:P1]] does not require exact time records where the employer's records fail to capture actual work. The initial factual showing nevertheless must permit a reasonable inference of the amount and extent of uncompensated work. Defendant submits that identifying the latest recorded transaction, without a reasoned estimate of actual work time, does not make that showing.

<!-- PAGE 5 -->
IV. THE SUPERVISOR'S INFORMATION
The May 13 reminder is addressed to the team and concerns quality of notes. It expressly contemplates notice to the lead, additional paid time or a handoff. MSG 1; SDEP 12–14. It does not direct employees to punch before finishing. Plaintiff acknowledges the absence of such an instruction and cannot identify a refused request for paid additional time on a disputed date. PDEP 14–15, 21–23. The supervisor could view queue information and request historical application events, but his ordinary screen did not reconcile those events with payroll. SDEP 6–11; IDEP 11–14. Possession of records in separate departments is not identical to contemporaneous knowledge that a particular employee performed unpaid work.

<!-- PAGE 6 -->
V. THE MAY EXCHANGE AND THE CORRECTION PROCEDURE
Plaintiff's May 20 message is evidence that she raised a concern. Defendant does not deny receipt. She identified no date, case number, or duration. MSG 2; SDEP 16–17. The next morning the supervisor directed her to stay on the clock, arrange a handoff as needed, send missed time to payroll, and offered assistance. MSG 3; SDEP 18–20. There is no written reply or specific correction request for these dates. A separate twelve-minute correction for a May 30 callback was approved and processed June 4. PAY 6; HDEP 12–13. Cadena v. Customer Connexx LLC, 107 F.4th 902 (9th Cir. 2024) (slip op. at 30–31 & n.16) [[AUTH:CAD:P1]] rejected summary judgment where evidence placed in dispute whether the correction practice permitted compensation for the activities at issue. Here the record contains a specific invitation to report and an actual example of correction.

<!-- PAGE 7 -->
VI. RELIEF
Defendant's position is not that failing to fill out a form constitutes a waiver of statutory rights. Under Forrester v. Roth's I.G.A. Foodliner, Inc., 646 F.2d 413, 414–15 (9th Cir. 1981) [[AUTH:FORE:P1]], the issue is whether the employer knew or should have known about the overtime despite the reporting failure. The individual message did not identify an event that could be matched with time records, and the response offered a means to clarify the concern. No evidence establishes that defendant refused a report or received the selected technical extract during the disputed period. On this record, defendant contends the evidence of both additional work and knowledge remains insufficient to submit liability to a factfinder. Defendant requests judgment on the single claim.
