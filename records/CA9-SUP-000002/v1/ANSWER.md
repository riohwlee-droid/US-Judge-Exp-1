STATEMENT OF THE CASE
The appeal concerns four weeks in spring 2024. The employee was paid forty hours each week at her regular rate. She alleges additional closing activities on four dates. The district court considered the records and five depositions and concluded that the evidence was insufficient on the extent of uncompensated work and employer knowledge (1-ER-4; 1-ER-8). The selected server records indicate accepted events, not duration. The employer's response to the employee's May message directed paid work and offered a way to correct time (2-ER-156). The record also documents a later correction for a different activity (2-ER-169). Appellee does not challenge the timeliness of the appeal or the finality of the judgment.

ARGUMENT
I. The initial evidentiary showing is not dispensed with by imperfect records.
29 U.S.C. § 207(a)(1) [[AUTH:STAT:P1]] addresses covered hours over forty in a workweek. The claim requires proof of some work beyond the paid hours and its amount and extent by a reasonable inference. Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co., 328 U.S. 680, 687–88 (1946) [[AUTH:AND:P1]] relaxes precision where records are inadequate, but does not dispense with the initial showing. Under Tolan v. Cotton, 572 U.S. 650, 656–57 (2014) [[AUTH:TOL:P1]], a court must accept justifiable inferences favoring the employee. It need not assume facts absent from the testimony. The employee disclaims a continuous interval from punch to save (2-ER-131; 2-ER-147). The custodian confirmed that the server timestamps cannot establish the duration of human interaction (2-ER-57; 2-ER-74). Appellee does not suggest that exact contemporaneous records are always required, but argues that the employee has not identified a grounded measure of time here.

<!-- PAGE 5 -->
The coworker described a common workflow and general observations without identifying any disputed date or out punch (2-ER-39; 2-ER-47). That evidence confirms the nature of closing tasks but does not fix even an approximate interval outside paid time. Appellant's reliance on Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co., 328 U.S. 680, 687–88 (1946) [[AUTH:AND:P1]] cannot convert a recorded difference in clock readings into evidence of the amount of active work. The employee recognizes that some text might have been composed earlier and that the later entry could involve a short submission (2-ER-130; 2-ER-132; 2-ER-133). The court did not require a precise number. It concluded the proposed estimates could not be derived from the evidence without speculation about how long any particular post-punch interaction lasted.

II. The evidence does not show actual or constructive knowledge of the claimed overtime.
Forrester v. Roth's I.G.A. Foodliner, Inc., 646 F.2d 413, 414–15 (9th Cir. 1981) [[AUTH:FORE:P1]] distinguishes an employer that knows or should know of work from one lacking the information necessary to discover it because the employee does not report it. A functioning procedure is relevant, although not conclusive. The employer did not instruct appellant to punch before finishing. The written reminder expressly contemplated paid extra time or a handoff (2-ER-154), and appellant acknowledged there was no technical requirement to punch precisely at five (2-ER-125). Neither witness identified a specific refused request for additional paid time. The supervisor was not shown to have observed one of the four operations after a known out punch. It was reasonable for the district court to distinguish ordinary pressure to complete records from actual or constructive notice of unpaid overtime.

<!-- PAGE 7 -->
The strongest evidence for appellant is her statement: "Sometimes I have a few minutes of notes after the clock says out." (2-ER-155). The supervisor replied the next morning, asked for dates and actual time for any missed work, told the employee not to work unpaid, and offered help. He did not deny a correction or insist that small amounts were uncompensable (2-ER-156; 2-ER-111; 2-ER-112). The wording of the employee's message did not identify a date, duration, or transaction. Forrester v. Roth's I.G.A. Foodliner, Inc., 646 F.2d 413, 414–15 (9th Cir. 1981) [[AUTH:FORE:P1]] does not require the employer to pay an amount it cannot determine, and appellee submits the response was an appropriate attempt to obtain information, not indifference to a known violation.

<!-- PAGE 8 -->
III. The correction mechanism and final event must be considered in context.
Cadena v. Customer Connexx LLC, 107 F.4th 902 (9th Cir. 2024) (slip op. at 30–31 & n.16) [[AUTH:CAD:P1]] involved a disputed practice concerning whether workers could obtain adjustments for the particular boot-up and shutdown activities. It expressly left the separate Forrester issue for remand. Here no evidence establishes a policy against payment for closing notes or an actual denial of a correction for that work. The employee successfully obtained a twelve-minute adjustment concerning a different callback (2-ER-169; 2-ER-86). Appellee acknowledges the correction is not proof that the earlier dates were paid. It is evidence of the availability and use of the procedure by this employee. The last selected event took place May 23 after the supervisor's directive to stay clocked in (2-ER-162). There is no evidence he observed the event while knowing the employee had punched out.

<!-- PAGE 9 -->
The employer could request technical information, but that possibility must be evaluated against what the report actually establishes. The selected exhibit was generated in January 2026 for litigation; it is not a contemporaneous warning presented to the supervisor (2-ER-69). The ordinary dashboard did not combine case-event timestamps and payroll punches (2-ER-65). A more extensive investigation might have been possible. The legal issue is reasonable diligence under Forrester v. Roth's I.G.A. Foodliner, Inc., 646 F.2d 413, 414–15 (9th Cir. 1981) [[AUTH:FORE:P1]], not whether, with the benefit of later litigation, one can imagine additional inquiries. The manager offered a practical method of obtaining the missing details from the employee herself. Appellant identifies no response supplying those details. Tolan v. Cotton, 572 U.S. 650, 656–57 (2014) [[AUTH:TOL:P1]] does not require an inference of constructive knowledge merely because additional information existed in separate systems.

<!-- PAGE 10 -->
CONCLUSION
The judgment should be affirmed. Appellee does not seek a categorical decision that application events can never corroborate a wage claim or that a complaint must use a particular form. It asks the Court to hold that the evidence here, even when considered in appellant's favor, does not satisfy the initial showing described by Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co., 328 U.S. 680, 687–88 (1946) [[AUTH:AND:P1]] and the knowledge requirement under Forrester v. Roth's I.G.A. Foodliner, Inc., 646 F.2d 413, 414–15 (9th Cir. 1981) [[AUTH:FORE:P1]]. If the Court finds the extent-of-work ground insufficient, the knowledge ground independently supports affirmance.
