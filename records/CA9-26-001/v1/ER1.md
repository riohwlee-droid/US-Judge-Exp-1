UNITED STATES DISTRICT COURT
NORTHERN DISTRICT OF CALIFORNIA — OAKLAND DIVISION
Maren v. Aster Quay Logistics, Inc. — SIM-NDCA-25-001 (fictional)
ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANT'S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT
Filed August 21, 2026.
The motion for summary judgment is granted. Plaintiff Elena R. Maren alleges that Aster Quay Logistics terminated her employment in retaliation for a complaint about possible sex discrimination in acting-lead assignments. The Court considers the sole retaliation claim and does not adjudicate the merits of the underlying assignment complaint. The parties agree for purposes of the motion that Plaintiff engaged in protected activity and experienced an adverse employment action. The issue is whether the record presents a genuine dispute on retaliatory causation. The Court has considered the memoranda, exhibits, deposition testimony and argument. It resolves no disputed testimony about the words spoken in March against Plaintiff; for the reasons below it concludes the existing evidence does not permit a reasonable jury to find that the protected activity was a but-for cause of discharge.

<!-- PAGE 3 -->
I. PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND
Plaintiff's charge identified retaliation following a written internal complaint and the termination. She received the notice of right to sue in December 2024 and filed this action in February 2025. The parties do not dispute coverage, venue or the timeliness of this claim for purposes of the motion. Discovery has closed. Defendant moved for summary judgment on May 4, 2026; Plaintiff opposed and Defendant replied. At the June 11 hearing neither party requested further discovery, and no separate motion to strike remained to be decided. The Court considers the cited evidence for the motion without making a trial admissibility determination beyond what is necessary here. The sole claim presents a federal question. This order resolves it in full. Damages issues, including mitigation, are not reached if the claim fails on causation.

<!-- PAGE 4 -->
II. SUMMARY JUDGMENT AND RETALIATION
The Court may grant judgment only when the nonmovant lacks evidence of a genuine material dispute and the movant is entitled to judgment under the governing law. Tolan v. Cotton, 572 U.S. 650, 656–57 (2014) [[AUTH:TOLAN:P1]]. The Court must credit the nonmovant's evidence to the extent its content can be presented in admissible form and draw reasonable inferences for her, without weighing competing testimony. For retaliation, the ultimate question is whether the protected activity was a but-for cause of the adverse action. University of Tex. Southwestern Medical Center v. Nassar, 570 U.S. 338, 360 (2013) [[AUTH:NASSAR:P1]]. Defendant articulates a legitimate explanation: recurring controlled-release documentation and authorization problems after prior instruction. The Court assumes that Plaintiff has established a prima facie case. The existence of such a case does not relieve her of the ultimate burden. The Court examines whether the evidence of pretext and the record as a whole permit a reasonable finding of the required causal connection.
The procedural standard is Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a) [[AUTH:FRCP56:P1]].
The Court uses, as an evidentiary tool rather than an additional element or a substitute for Rule 56, the familiar allocation of burdens in McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802–04 (1973) [[AUTH:MCD:P1]] without transferring the ultimate burden of persuasion to Defendant.

III. THE UNDISPUTED AND DISPUTED RECORD
The initial common review followed a customer concern in early April and preceded Plaintiff's written complaint. The first report covered six coordinators. The manager learned of Plaintiff's complaint on April 9 and requested a sixty-day review of her transactions on April 12. Quality's later correction resolved eight of twelve raw flags, leaving four open. The Court does not treat the four open entries as four proven unauthorized releases. Two involve disputed authorization; the remaining two concern incomplete documentation. The policy permits oral authorization in qualifying urgent cases but still requires a complete note. Plaintiff had been coached about that requirement in November 2023. The Court assumes for purposes of the motion that she received oral authorization for both disputed consignments. The record defects identified by the corrected report are not thereby eliminated. Plaintiff does not identify complete timely documentation for the disputed loads.
Record references: AUD pp. 1–5; COMP p. 3; LOG pp. 5–6, 9–10; POL pp. 3–4; REV p. 3; MDEP pp. 6–9; ADEP pp. 7–9.

IV. SCOPE OF REVIEW AND TEMPORAL PROXIMITY
The individual expansion was made after the manager learned of the complaint, and the manager does not identify a written plan for that expansion before the complaint. The Court accepts that sequence as evidence favorable to Plaintiff. The governing case law does not impose a temporal bright line. Kama concerns federal employment; this Court uses its analysis of circumstantial pretext evidence, not as a replacement for Nassar's private-sector causation standard. Kama v. Mayorkas, 107 F.4th 1054 (9th Cir. 2024), slip op. at 13 [[AUTH:KAMA:P1]]. The question is whether the timing and other evidence refute the proffered explanation sufficiently to permit an inference of retaliatory causation. The common review was independently triggered. The prior coaching was about documentation, and the expanded report identified actual unresolved documentation defects. The Court concludes that the fact that the manager selected Plaintiff for a broader review, without more evidence undermining the stated rationale of recurrence after prior instruction, does not establish the requisite genuine issue. The Court does not hold that a selectively directed investigation can never be evidence of retaliation.
Record references: SDEP pp. 4–7, 16; AUD pp. 1–3; REV p. 3.
The Court considers the illustration in Curley v. City of North Las Vegas, 772 F.3d 629, 634 (9th Cir. 2014) [[AUTH:CURLEY:P1]] that a genuinely independent investigation may weaken the inference from timing. It does not hold that discovery of actual defects always forecloses evidence that the investigation itself was retaliatorily directed.

V. COMPARATOR AND AUTHORIZATION EVIDENCE
Plaintiff identifies Bellford, a coordinator subject to the same policy and manager, who received a warning after the common review. The common and expanded windows are not quantitatively comparable, and no sixty-day Bellford result is in this record. The Court accepts that Bellford's customer shipment had an incomplete record and that the company accepted a later report of lead permission. Plaintiff's own lead did not deny giving permission but could not definitively identify the two loads. The Court assumes the permission occurred, and recognizes the difference in the inquiry as a circumstance favorable to Plaintiff. The record nevertheless identifies prior individual coaching in Plaintiff's file and four open entries in the corrected review. The Court concludes that the comparator evidence does not materially undermine the company's stated distinction: recurring documentation problems after prior instruction. It would require speculation about the unknown longer comparator record to infer that the two histories were equivalent.
Record references: WARN pp. 1–4; CDEP pp. 3–7, 10; WDEP pp. 4–8, 10; SDEP pp. 9–12; HDEP pp. 8–9.

VI. THE DECISION PROCESS AND THE WHOLE RECORD
The April 25 recommendation, HR's questions, the April 30 response and the COO note are not identical in phrasing. The Court does not treat HR review as an automatic bar to attribution of a recommending manager's motive. But the documents consistently address the corrected exceptions and prior coaching, and the final notice explicitly excludes the preliminary count. The manager's statement that documentation mattered even assuming oral permission predates the final decision. Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., 530 U.S. 133, 148 (2000) [[AUTH:REEVES:P1]] addresses an ADEA verdict and recognizes the potential evidentiary significance of discrediting an asserted justification, but also leaves room for judgment as a matter of law depending on the whole record. Even considering timing, differential scope, comparator discipline and the authorization evidence together, the Court concludes that Plaintiff has not sufficiently undermined the contemporaneously documented rationale to allow a reasonable finding that the complaint made the difference to the discharge. This conclusion is one of evidentiary sufficiency, not a finding that Plaintiff's description of the conversations is untrue.
Record references: TERM pp. 1–6; HDEP pp. 9–13; SDEP pp. 10–13; HEAR pp. 3–7.
Poland v. Chertoff, 494 F.3d 1174, 1182 (9th Cir. 2007) [[AUTH:POLAND:P1]] recognizes the relevance of a biased subordinate's influence. The Court does not adopt a categorical independence rule and assesses the sufficiency of evidence of retaliatory motive and causation on the whole record.

<!-- PAGE 9 -->
VII. DISPOSITION
For the foregoing reasons, Defendant's motion is GRANTED. This disposition is limited to the sole Title VII retaliation claim and the particular summary judgment record before the Court. It does not determine whether the underlying lead-assignment complaint was well founded or declare a general immunity for discretionary personnel investigations. The Clerk shall enter separate judgment for Defendant and close this case. Any request for costs shall be made in accordance with applicable procedures. No issue of attorney fees or taxable costs is adjudicated in this order. The Court makes no determination of the amount of recoverable damages because liability has not been established.
IT IS SO ORDERED.
