DEFENDANT'S REPLY IN SUPPORT OF MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT
Plaintiff's opposition narrows the dispute in an important respect. She does not challenge the initial common review, deny the existence of the corrected open entries, or request more discovery. She asks the Court to infer that the manager expanded her audit because of protected activity and used the results to secure a discharge. That inference requires more than the fact that the manager knew of the complaint when he performed an otherwise permissible act. Defendant submits that the actual documentary record explains the expansion by reference to prior coaching and recurring preliminary entries and explains discharge by reference to the corrected record. The same documentation defects remain even if the two oral approvals are assumed to have occurred.

<!-- PAGE 10 -->
I. THE CAUSATION REQUIREMENT CANNOT BE REPLACED WITH AN AUDIT-FAIRNESS TEST
University of Tex. Southwestern Medical Center v. Nassar, 570 U.S. 338, 360 (2013) [[AUTH:NASSAR:P1]] requires a causal connection to the challenged adverse action. The only adverse action alleged is discharge. The Court may consider the scope of the audit as evidence relevant to that action, but it should not impose a uniform-audit requirement as though it were an independent statutory element. Plaintiff has no evidence of the actual results an extended comparator review would have produced. Her inference therefore depends in part on a possibility not established in this record. The evidence shows prior individual coaching for Maren, a factual reason to examine recurrence, and actual unreconciled documentation. Even if a different scope would have been fairer, the inference of retaliatory causation does not follow automatically.

<!-- PAGE 11 -->
II. THE AUTHORIZATION TESTIMONY DOES NOT NEGATE THE DOCUMENTATION BASIS
The lead did not affirmatively deny the exchanges, but she could not match both shipment numbers with certainty. The manager distinguished that response from the specific confirmation reported for Bellford. Plaintiff is correct that the policy does not require a signed statement; that observation supports rather than refutes the employer's ability to evaluate information received informally. The final recommendation need not depend on resolving whether permission was given. The documentation requirement remained, and the earlier coaching concerned that very requirement. The termination notice and COO decision identify the corrected basis. They do not state that the employee was terminated because there were twelve proven violations. There is no evidence that Quality altered the underlying rows or that the approving official believed the preliminary count remained the operative count.

<!-- PAGE 12 -->
III. THE TIMING EVIDENCE MUST BE CONSIDERED WITH THE INDEPENDENT EXPLANATION
In Kama v. Mayorkas, 107 F.4th 1054 (9th Cir. 2024), slip op. at 13 [[AUTH:KAMA:P1]], timing was insufficient on the particular record in light of the alternative explanation and other evidence. Defendant recognizes that the case does not establish an absolute temporal rule. It submits that the contemporaneous operational explanation here is substantial: the common review preceded the complaint, the prior coaching is documented, the expanded query found actual open entries and the final packet acknowledged the limits of the evidence. A factfinder may reject an employer's reason when evidence warrants that rejection; Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., 530 U.S. 133, 148 (2000) [[AUTH:REEVES:P1]] does not make every disputed managerial assessment sufficient evidence of unlawful causation. Plaintiff has not shown that the corrected reason was false. Her argument concerns the wisdom and completeness of the process rather than sufficient evidence that retaliation made the difference.

<!-- PAGE 13 -->
CONCLUSION
Defendant requests judgment on the sole claim. It does not ask the Court to resolve whether Maren's recollections about oral permission are true or to discredit the lead's testimony. It asks the Court to assume the favorable version of those disputed events and decide whether the record nevertheless fails to permit a reasonable finding of but-for causation. If the Court finds a material dispute on that question, the proper course is trial rather than an adverse credibility finding on the papers.

PLAINTIFF'S OPPOSITION TO DEFENDANT'S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT
A reasonable jury could find that the protected complaint was a but-for cause of discharge. Plaintiff does not dispute that the company was entitled to investigate genuine release problems, nor does she ask this Court to hold that the earlier common review itself was retaliatory. The question is whether the employer used the separately expanded review and discretionary disciplinary decision to impose termination that would not otherwise have occurred. The evidentiary record includes the manager's dated instructions, the corrected report, the different review scope of a comparator, the witnesses' accounts of oral approvals, and the approval process. A jury must be permitted to consider those circumstances together. Plaintiff relies on the existing record and does not request additional discovery or seek to add a claim concerning the underlying allocation of lead shifts.

<!-- PAGE 15 -->
I. THE APPLICABLE STANDARD
Plaintiff accepts the but-for standard articulated in University of Tex. Southwestern Medical Center v. Nassar, 570 U.S. 338, 360 (2013) [[AUTH:NASSAR:P1]]. A reasonable inference that the employee would not have been discharged absent retaliation satisfies the factual question at trial; it does not require Plaintiff to establish that no operational concern existed. The question today is whether that inference is available to a reasonable jury. Tolan v. Cotton, 572 U.S. 650, 656–57 (2014) [[AUTH:TOLAN:P1]]. A court may not select the employer's explanation because it considers it more convincing when the record supports a contrary reasonable inference. The company's proof of the existence of documentation defects does not by itself answer the distinct question whether those defects actually led to discharge absent the complaint. A decision can have multiple factual inputs and still depend on an unlawful one. Plaintiff does not seek a motivating-factor instruction or a reversal of the ultimate burden of persuasion.

<!-- PAGE 16 -->
II. THE COMMON REVIEW AND THE SELECTIVE EXPANSION ARE DIFFERENT DECISIONS
The customer complaint concerned Bellford, not Maren. The first report showed three preliminary flags for Maren and four for Bellford. AUD, pp. 1–2. On April 9 the manager learned the nature of the protected complaint. COMP, p. 3; SDEP, p. 5. Three days later he asked Quality to examine only Maren over sixty days. AUD, p. 3. He cannot identify a written precomplaint plan for that specific expansion. SDEP, pp. 4, 16. The November coaching note was available before the common review and concerned a documentation issue for which permission was confirmed. REV, p. 3. The manager requested the expansion before Quality had determined whether the three preliminary flags were violations. SDEP, p. 6. A jury could accept that this sequence demonstrates a permissible focus on recurrence. It could also infer that the complaint changed the intensity of the scrutiny imposed on Maren.

<!-- PAGE 17 -->
III. THE ANALYST WARNED THAT THE COUNTS WERE NOT COMPARABLE
The analyst did not recommend reviewing only Maren. He warned that the sixty-day report would not be directly comparable to the common-window counts. AUD, p. 3; ADEP, p. 4. He explained that no verified denominator of eligible releases was included. ADEP, p. 3. Plaintiff does not ask the Court to infer an unknown count for Bellford. The relevant evidence is the manager's selection of an unequal period of scrutiny and his subsequent reliance on recurrence as a reason to distinguish discipline. A difference created by the selection process should not be treated as an independent objective distinction without examining how it arose. If an employer finds more issues by inspecting one employee over a substantially longer period, that finding can be genuine while the use of the investigation in a disciplinary decision is still contested. This argument addresses causation rather than a freestanding duty to conduct identical audits.

<!-- PAGE 18 -->
IV. CORRECTION OF THE COUNT DOES NOT ANSWER THE MOTIVE QUESTION
Quality resolved eight of twelve preliminary flags and retained four. AUD, p. 5. Plaintiff accepts the corrected number. She does not allege that the analyst falsified the electronic records. What matters is how management characterized the remaining entries and evaluated the employee's explanation. The procedure distinguishes an unauthorized release from an incomplete note. POL, p. 3. Maren identified the lead and the relevant circumstances at the April 18 interview. COMP, p. 4. The manager did not obtain a signed statement from the lead, did not recall a detailed interview and admitted the absence of confirmation was different from an affirmative denial. SDEP, pp. 8–9. The lead testified that the circumstances were consistent with her recollections of oral instructions, although she could not definitively match shipment numbers. WDEP, pp. 4–8. These are not findings of full compliance. They bear on the asserted gravity and decision process.

<!-- PAGE 19 -->
V. COMPARATOR EVIDENCE IS QUALITATIVE AS WELL AS QUANTITATIVE
Bellford received a warning for two open entries, including the customer consignment. WARN, pp. 1–2. His release screen showed an incomplete destination field and his manual note was incomplete. LOG, p. 11. Management accepted a later report of specific lead confirmation. It did not require a written statement for that confirmation. SDEP, p. 12; CDEP, p. 4. Maren's earlier coaching was not a formal warning. HDEP, p. 8. Bellford recalled ordinary reminders and an individual instruction concerning a missing reason field, though he did not recall a comparable formal coaching record. CDEP, p. 6. The employees are not identical and Plaintiff does not pretend that they are. Their common manager, duties and rules, and the different inquiry into authorization and recurrence, provide evidence that can be weighed alongside timing. The question is not whether the Court would have given the same sanction to each.

<!-- PAGE 20 -->
VI. THE CONTEMPORANEOUS DECISION DOCUMENTS SUPPORT TWO PERMISSIBLE INFERENCES
The April 25 recommendation mentioned both disputed authorization and documentation. TERM, p. 1. HR expressly asked whether the evidence showed lack of oral approval or only lack of a record, and sought comparator information. TERM, p. 2. The manager subsequently described documentation as serious even if a conversation occurred. TERM, p. 3. The COO accepted that view while acknowledging that he did not independently interview the lead or re-run the audit. TERM, p. 5. This is evidence of review, but it does not compel a finding that the original selection or recommendation was unaffected by the complaint. The approving official relied on the recommendation of the manager who had learned of the complaint and had selected the expanded window. The jury could find that the documentation rationale was genuine but insufficient on its own to explain the sanction; the decision-maker's assertion that the complaint was excluded does not automatically foreclose that inference.

<!-- PAGE 21 -->
VII. PRETEXT MUST BE EVALUATED ON THE WHOLE RECORD
Kama v. Mayorkas, 107 F.4th 1054 (9th Cir. 2024), slip op. at 13 [[AUTH:KAMA:P1]] does not establish that timing can never contribute to pretext, nor does Plaintiff rely on timing alone. The distinctive evidence here concerns a manager who was informed of the complaint, made the individualized review request shortly thereafter and supplied the recommendation that became the basis of discharge. The independent initial audit is relevant, but unlike a documented individual termination process already underway, it does not resolve the later choice. Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., 530 U.S. 133, 148 (2000) [[AUTH:REEVES:P1]] illustrates why evidence undermining an asserted explanation can support an inference of an unlawful motive while leaving the ultimate decision to the factfinder. Plaintiff does not treat an age-discrimination case as modifying the Title VII retaliation causation standard. She invokes the evidentiary reasoning in combination with the specific standard imposed by Nassar.

<!-- PAGE 22 -->
VIII. NO REQUEST TO TRY THE CASE ON THIS MOTION
A jury might credit the manager's testimony that prior coaching and recurring record defects warranted discharge regardless of protected activity. It might accept the comparator distinctions and HR's review as adequate explanations. That is a possible trial outcome, not a reason to remove the case from the jury if the competing inference is reasonable. Plaintiff likewise does not demand that the Court find the employer liable. The Court need only deny judgment where the record permits the jury to find that the protected complaint made a difference to the scope of scrutiny, the evaluation of the record, and ultimately the level of discipline. The dispute is not about whether the four entries existed; it is about the reason for selecting and treating the record as the basis for discharge.

DEFENDANT'S NOTICE OF MOTION AND MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT
Hearing June 11, 2026, 10:00 a.m., before Judge Rosa L. Vellum. Defendant moves for judgment on the sole retaliation claim. Defendant relies on the accompanying record exhibits A through I and the deposition testimony identified in this memorandum. For purposes of the motion, Defendant assumes Plaintiff engaged in protected opposition and that discharge was materially adverse. The issue is whether evidence would permit a reasonable jury to find the complaint was a necessary cause of discharge. Defendant does not seek a finding that every preliminary exception was a violation or that the assignment complaint itself was false. The requested judgment turns on causation, including the contemporaneous evidence of a distinct operational basis and the absence of sufficient evidence that this basis was a cover for retaliation.

<!-- PAGE 61 -->
MEMORANDUM — I. STANDARD AND QUESTION PRESENTED
The substantive causation requirement is set by University of Tex. Southwestern Medical Center v. Nassar, 570 U.S. 338, 360 (2013) [[AUTH:NASSAR:P1]]. Plaintiff must establish that her protected conduct made the difference to the adverse action, rather than merely identifying an impermissible consideration that may have been present without affecting the result. On summary judgment the Court must not decide genuine material factual disputes against the nonmoving party. Tolan v. Cotton, 572 U.S. 650, 656–57 (2014) [[AUTH:TOLAN:P1]]. Defendant accepts that distinction and does not ask the Court to determine whether Maren or the lead has the more accurate memory. For this motion, assume the oral approval conversations occurred. The documents still show incomplete contemporaneous records. The relevant question is whether evidence permits a reasonable inference that the company would have retained her absent the complaint, not whether a different manager could have imposed a warning.

<!-- PAGE 62 -->
II. THE CONTROL REQUIREMENT WAS KNOWN AND PREEXISTING
The operative policy expressly distinguishes urgent oral permission from the requirement to make a complete note. Exhibit A (POL), pp. 2–4. The company does not equate every blank in an export with lack of authorization. The policy instead requires reconciliation and permits discipline according to the circumstances. Maren acknowledges that she could not authorize her own exceptions and that an urgent approval required a completed note by the end of the shift. Maren Dep. (MDEP), pp. 2, 7–9. The November 2023 coaching note specifically concerned a missing lead name and informed her of the importance of the documentation requirement. Exhibit B (REV), p. 3. Her February assessment remained satisfactory. This is not an inconsistency: a satisfactory overall rating does not erase a documented instruction about a particular procedure, much less excuse later failures to follow it.

<!-- PAGE 63 -->
III. THE COMMON AUDIT HAD AN INDEPENDENT TRIGGER
On April 2 a customer complained about a load handled by Bellford. The April 4 request directed Quality to examine a common fourteen-day period for six coordinators, before Maren's written complaint to HR. Exhibit C (AUD), pp. 1–2; Holtwell Dep. (SDEP), pp. 3–4. The common file was preserved. That is objective evidence that the operational issue was genuine and did not originate as a response to protected opposition. Defendant acknowledges the separate request for a sixty-day Maren review was made on April 12 after the manager learned of the complaint. The reason identified in the request itself was a concern about recurrence in light of earlier coaching. Exhibit C, p. 3. The fact that the request came later than notice of the complaint does not render the reason false. The relevant facts concerning the coaching and preliminary flags existed already.

<!-- PAGE 64 -->
IV. THE CORRECTED REPORT, NOT THE RAW COUNT, UNDERLAY THE DECISION
The expanded query used the same filter over a longer period. Exhibit D (LOG), pp. 2–3. Of twelve preliminary entries, Quality resolved eight and left four open. Exhibit C, pp. 4–5. Two involved disputed authorization and two incomplete documentation. Quality did not purport to establish what was said orally. Fenmore Dep. (ADEP), pp. 7–9. The company accepted these limits in the recommendation, the HR worksheet and the decision note. Exhibit I (TERM), pp. 1, 4–6. The record therefore supplies no basis to infer that the employer fabricated twelve violations or fired Maren on that premise. The distinction between a real record defect and the underlying oral permission matters, but it does not eliminate the documented defect itself. The Court should evaluate the actual stated reason rather than a more extreme reason Plaintiff does not show the decision-maker adopted.

<!-- PAGE 65 -->
V. EVEN IF ORAL APPROVAL OCCURRED, THE RECORD DEFECTS REMAIN
AQ-4182 lacks a complete timely reference. AQ-4311 contains a manual reference with a blank lead field. Exhibit D, pp. 9–10. Maren does not identify a retained completed note for the former, and she does not dispute the missing name in the latter. MDEP, pp. 7–9. The corrected report also identifies two other incomplete entries. A manager could regard repeated documentation failures after earlier coaching as warranting discharge because the company needs to reconstruct the authorization process when an error is alleged. The policy gives management discretion, not an automatic sanction. A court need not conclude that discharge was wise or proportionate. It must determine whether there is evidence that the proffered explanation did not actually motivate the action and that the protected activity made the necessary difference. The contemporaneous records directly address that explanation.

<!-- PAGE 66 -->
VI. TEMPORAL SEQUENCE DOES NOT ESTABLISH PRETEXT ON THIS RECORD
The Court of Appeals has recognized that timing can be probative but held that timing alone was insufficient on the particular record before it in Kama v. Mayorkas, 107 F.4th 1054 (9th Cir. 2024), slip op. at 13 [[AUTH:KAMA:P1]]. Its reasoning requires attention to evidence of an independent basis for the action. Here the customer concern, common review, prior coaching and actual open entries are independent evidence, not solely a retrospective justification. Plaintiff cannot erase those facts by observing that her individual review was expanded after the complaint. Defendant does not urge a bright-line rule based on the number of days. Rather, it submits that the timing fits an ongoing operational review, and the other circumstances alleged do not materially undermine the explanation. A jury could disagree with the scope of a discretionary audit without reasonably finding that the protected complaint caused the resulting discharge.

<!-- PAGE 67 -->
VII. COMPARISON DOES NOT ESTABLISH EQUIVALENT CIRCUMSTANCES
The common review was a screening exercise, not a controlled comparison of discipline. It did not contain a denominator of eligible releases, and the longer Maren window is not comparable numerically to the fourteen-day Bellford window. ADEP, pp. 3–4, 10. Defendant does not infer from this absence that Bellford had a flawless earlier record. Rather, the absence means Plaintiff cannot demonstrate an equivalent pattern of unresolved items in the two employees' actual reviewed records. Holtwell testified that the warning addressed a customer load for which the lead had given a specific confirmation and an additional documentation entry, whereas Maren's permission remained disputed and she had documented prior coaching. SDEP, pp. 7, 12, 15. These differences are relevant to the decision, even though both coordinators were governed by the same policy.

<!-- PAGE 68 -->
VIII. THE FINAL DECISION AND THE EVIDENTIARY BURDEN
HR expressly asked for corrected data and comparison information, and the COO approved discharge on the corrected record and prior instruction. TERM, pp. 2–5. The approval is not offered as a categorical defense to any possible improper motive of a recommending manager. It is evidence of a contemporaneous review that did not accept a knowingly inflated count. Plaintiff may use circumstantial evidence to challenge an employer's stated reason; Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., 530 U.S. 133, 148 (2000) [[AUTH:REEVES:P1]], addressing an age-discrimination verdict, recognizes that discrediting a justification can support an inference of discrimination but does not compel it. In this retaliation case the final causation requirement remains the more specific rule of Nassar. Plaintiff has not presented evidence discrediting the corrected documentation basis sufficiently to support that necessary inference. The case is not a referendum on the completeness of HR's investigation.

<!-- PAGE 69 -->
CONCLUSION AND REQUEST FOR RELIEF
The Court should enter judgment for Aster Quay Logistics, Inc. on the sole retaliation claim. In doing so the Court need not determine the truth of the underlying assignment complaint, declare that all preliminary exceptions were violations, or find that Maren never received oral permission. The relevant question is the sufficiency of the evidence of but-for causation in the existing record. Defendant contends that the undisputed documentation defects, prior coaching and independently triggered review, considered with the actual decision materials, permit no reasonable finding of retaliatory discharge. If the Court determines that a genuine dispute of material fact remains as to the reason for discharge, Defendant recognizes that this motion must be denied rather than resolved through a credibility determination.

ASTER QUAY LOGISTICS — OPERATIONS CONTROL MANUAL
Document AQ-OPS-17; revision 3; effective January 8, 2024
Subject: Controlled dispatch release. Owner: Operations. Applies to all Oakland dispatch coordinators and shift leads.

1. Purpose. A dispatch release permits a carrier to collect a consignment at the assigned door. The release must establish that identity, consignee and loading checks were completed. A release is not the same as the later departure scan. The distinction matters where a carrier is at the facility but a shipment is held for a documentation discrepancy. Coordinators are responsible for confirming the release status and for retaining a contemporaneous approval trail. Shift leads are responsible for decisions to permit exceptions. A coordinator may not use another employee's account or enter an approval in the name of a lead who has not given that approval.

<!-- PAGE 71 -->
2. Standard workflow. The coordinator checks the order number against the carrier's collection instruction, verifies the destination field, reviews the release screen for a complete green status, and sends the release message. The system records the account, terminal, event time and approval field. A completed status means that the release is permitted under the ordinary workflow. An incomplete field requires the coordinator to stop and contact the shift lead. A late synchronization of an approval may appear in the reporting export as a blank field at the moment of release even when the approval has been captured elsewhere. For that reason the exception report is a screening tool, not a final disciplinary determination. The reviewing employee should examine the field history and any corroborating message or manual log before closing an exception.

<!-- PAGE 72 -->
3. Urgent exception. A shift lead may authorize an urgent release orally when the ordinary electronic approval cannot be entered at the time of release and the identity and destination checks are complete. The coordinator must record the lead's identity, the time and the reason before the end of the shift. The lead should confirm that record during the next available review period. A delay in the subsequent synchronization of a timely manual entry is not itself an unauthorized release. An oral instruction not documented before the end of the shift is a documentation exception even if approval was actually given. A release made without required authorization is a substantive control violation. A request from a carrier or customer is not authorization. The coordinator must not treat silence by a lead as affirmative permission.

<!-- PAGE 73 -->
4. Review and discipline. Quality staff may select a sample of transactions, reconcile suspected synchronization defects and refer unresolved items to operations. The initial exception count must not be used as the final count without reconciliation. Review may be expanded where a documented recurring problem suggests a broader pattern. An expanded review should identify its reason and time window. Operations may counsel, warn, suspend or recommend discharge depending on seriousness, recurrence, prior notice and surrounding circumstances. No fixed number of exceptions automatically requires discharge. HR reviews proposed discharge for policy consistency and relevant comparable discipline. Management retains discretion subject to applicable law and the obligation not to retaliate for a protected complaint. The review and discipline decisions should be documented separately from any complaint investigation.

<!-- PAGE 74 -->

Revision 1 — initial release control instructions, 2022. Revision 2 — manual log and synchronization clarification, 2023. Revision 3 — January 8, 2024, clarifies that oral approval is permitted in qualifying urgent cases and that quality reporting is a screening tool. Distribution: all dispatch coordinators, all shift leads, dispatch manager, quality analyst and HR policy mailbox. Acknowledgments retained in the learning system.
