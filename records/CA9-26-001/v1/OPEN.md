ISSUE PRESENTED
Whether the record, viewed in Appellant's favor, would permit a reasonable jury to find that her opposition to perceived sex discrimination was a but-for cause of discharge, considering the altered audit scope, comparative discipline and the employer's evaluation of disputed oral authorizations.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE — EMPLOYMENT AND COMPLAINT
Maren worked at Aster Quay's Oakland facility. The policy permits urgent oral approval but requires a complete record by shift end. 2-ER-81. She received coaching about an incomplete note in November 2023; the lead confirmed authorization on that earlier transaction. 2-ER-86. Her overall annual assessment was meets expectations. 2-ER-84. She complained to HR on April 8, 2024, about possible sex discrimination in acting-lead assignments. 2-ER-32. HR told her manager the nature of the complaint on April 9 and instructed him to preserve records and keep the operational inquiry separate. 2-ER-34. The parties treated this complaint as protected opposition for purposes of summary judgment.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT
The order identifies the correct ultimate causation standard but removes a reasonable inferential dispute from the jury. An independently triggered common audit does not necessarily explain the subsequent individualized extension or the sanction. Prior coaching provides a possible legitimate explanation, but its subject and the timing of its invocation permit another inference. The company's reliance on real documentation problems need not make discharge inevitable. The comparator and the different treatment of the oral authorization evidence bear on whether those problems would have resulted in termination in the absence of protected opposition. Appellant does not ask the Court to infer an unknown number of comparator violations or to determine the truth of the underlying discrimination complaint. She seeks a trial on the preserved retaliation claim.

STANDARD OF REVIEW
Review of summary judgment is de novo. The Court must credit the nonmovant's evidence and reasonable inferences rather than resolve genuine material factual disputes against her. Tolan v. Cotton, 572 U.S. 650, 656–57 (2014) [[AUTH:TOLAN:P1]]. Materiality is determined by the substantive law: University of Tex. Southwestern Medical Center v. Nassar, 570 U.S. 338, 360 (2013) [[AUTH:NASSAR:P1]] requires proof that protected activity made the necessary difference to the adverse action. Appellant accepts that ultimate burden. At this stage the Court asks whether a reasonable jury could draw that inference, not whether the employer's alternative explanation appears more persuasive. Judgment for an employer remains proper if no reasonable jury could find the required causal relationship.
The summary judgment standard derives from Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a) [[AUTH:FRCP56:P1]].
The burden-shifting framework, McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802–04 (1973) [[AUTH:MCD:P1]], does not require the employee at summary judgment to persuade the court that the employer's rationale is false; the question is whether the evidence permits the jury to do so and find the ultimate causal fact.

ARGUMENT — I. THE COUNTERFACTUAL CONCERNS DISCHARGE
University of Tex. Southwestern Medical Center v. Nassar, 570 U.S. 338, 360 (2013) [[AUTH:NASSAR:P1]]. The customer complaint and April 4 common audit are undisputed. 2-ER-88. The manager could identify no precomplaint written plan to examine Maren alone over sixty days; the April 12 email is the written request. 2-ER-125, 2-ER-137. A jury could find that without the complaint the common-window entries would have been reconciled and considered without the targeted historical query. The fact that the expanded query accurately identified record defects does not answer why it was directed at her. This is not a freestanding claim that the audit was a separate adverse action; the selection is evidence bearing on why the discharge occurred. A jury may credit the stated concern about recurrence, but it is not compelled to do so.

<!-- PAGE 11 -->
II. RECURRENCE WAS INVOKED BEFORE COMPARABLE RECURRENCE WAS MEASURED
The common report did not identify Maren as having the most raw flags. The manager requested the extension before Quality had resolved her preliminary entries. 2-ER-125, 2-ER-127. No policy required a sixty-day extension whenever a coaching note existed. 2-ER-82. Quality expressly warned about comparability. 2-ER-90. Appellant is not asking the Court to assume that an equal window would yield equal counts. The unequal scope is evidence about how the employer generated the very information it later cited to distinguish discipline. Tolan v. Cotton, 572 U.S. 650, 656–57 (2014) [[AUTH:TOLAN:P1]]. The inference concerns the decision to select the scope, not the unknown result of a different search. A reasonable jury could regard that selection as important to the counterfactual.

<!-- PAGE 12 -->
III. THE DOCUMENTATION DEFECTS AND THE SANCTION ARE DISTINCT
The field history does not establish complete notes for the disputed loads. 2-ER-102, 2-ER-103. Maren acknowledges those blanks. 2-ER-112, 2-ER-113. But the policy distinguishes an incomplete record from an unauthorized release. 2-ER-81. Lorne recalled circumstances consistent with permission and did not affirmatively deny the two approvals. 2-ER-40, 2-ER-42, 2-ER-44. The manager's recommendation referred to both authorization and documentation; later he emphasized documentation even assuming a conversation occurred. 2-ER-166, 2-ER-168. Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., 530 U.S. 133, 148 (2000) [[AUTH:REEVES:P1]]. Reeves addressed an ADEA verdict under Rule 50. Its evidentiary reasoning is invoked here by analogy; it does not displace Nassar's causation rule or compel a finding of liability. Evidence undermining the explanation for a particular sanction can be probative even though an employer identifies an actual concern. Appellant does not ask a jury to find the defects fabricated.

<!-- PAGE 13 -->
IV. THE COMPARATOR AND TIMING EVIDENCE MUST BE CONSIDERED TOGETHER
Kama v. Mayorkas, 107 F.4th 1054 (9th Cir. 2024), slip op. at 13 [[AUTH:KAMA:P1]]. That decision assessed a federal employee's retaliation claim, a 56-day interval, and independently documented noncooperation. Appellant relies on its evidentiary treatment of pretext, not any assumption that federal- and private-sector causation standards are identical. It did not make temporal proximity categorically irrelevant. Here the individual review followed notice of the complaint by three days. The customer problem involved Bellford, whose later lead confirmation was accepted without a signed statement. 2-ER-88, 2-ER-90, 2-ER-133. His documentation remained incomplete and the destination field was pending at release. 2-ER-104. Maren's earlier coaching and the different specificity of the accounts are relevant distinctions. A jury could consider their significance alongside the selective investigation and different inquiry into permission. Appellant does not contend that identical jobs alone compel identical sanctions.
Dawson v. Entek International, 630 F.3d 928, 937 (9th Cir. 2011) [[AUTH:DAWSON:P1]] illustrates that timing together with other evidence of retaliatory motive can permit trial. Here the short interval is from notice to the challenged selection, not from notice to the discharge; Appellant relies on the relationship of that selection to the final action.
Curley v. City of North Las Vegas, 772 F.3d 629, 634 (9th Cir. 2014) [[AUTH:CURLEY:P1]] involved genuinely intervening information from an investigation; the present dispute concerns whether the individual decision to expand the review itself was influenced by the complaint. Appellant does not contend that the resulting entries were fabricated.

<!-- PAGE 14 -->
V. THE COO'S REVIEW DOES NOT MAKE THE MANAGER'S MOTIVE IMMATERIAL
The manager supplied the operational recommendation. The COO considered the corrected file but did not independently interview Lorne or rerun the audit. 2-ER-169, 2-ER-170. HR asked about the distinction between absence of permission and absence of a record and about comparable treatment. 2-ER-167. Those documents are substantial evidence for Appellee; they also demonstrate that the questions were not independently resolved. Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., 530 U.S. 133, 148 (2000) [[AUTH:REEVES:P1]]. The approving official's involvement does not categorically answer whether the manager's selection and recommendation made the necessary difference. A jury might accept the independent rationale in full. Because it could reasonably draw the opposite causal inference from the combined circumstances, the order should not foreclose trial.
Poland v. Chertoff, 494 F.3d 1174, 1182 (9th Cir. 2007) [[AUTH:POLAND:P1]] recognizes that an independent decisionmaker's involvement is not dispositive if the subordinate's bias influenced the decision or process. Appellant must still prove the bias and the necessary causal connection; the contention is that the manager's role is material evidence on this record.
