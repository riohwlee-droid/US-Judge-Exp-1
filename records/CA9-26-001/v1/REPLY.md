ARGUMENT — I. THE COMMON AUDIT DOES NOT ANSWER THE LATER COUNTERFACTUAL
Appellee accepts that the individualized extension followed notice of the complaint. The common review was requested April 4 and the extension April 12. 2-ER-88, 2-ER-90. Kama v. Mayorkas, 107 F.4th 1054 (9th Cir. 2024), slip op. at 13 [[AUTH:KAMA:P1]]. Appellant does not argue that timing alone suffices or that the investigation was a separate adverse action. The extension is evidence of the process that generated the reason for discharge. University of Tex. Southwestern Medical Center v. Nassar, 570 U.S. 338, 360 (2013) [[AUTH:NASSAR:P1]]. The prior coaching existed when the common review was ordered and concerned incomplete documentation. The timing and selective reliance on that coaching are circumstances from which a jury could decide whether the complaint made a difference to the eventual sanction. A documented reason is not immune from challenge concerning its actual role.

<!-- PAGE 5 -->
II. APPELLANT DOES NOT ASK THE COURT TO SUPPLY UNKNOWN DATA
Bellford received a written warning. 2-ER-62. His sixty-day count is unknown. That absence does not require a jury to disregard the evidence of different periods of scrutiny and the different inquiries into permission. The inference concerns the selection decision and qualitative comparison, not the results of an unperformed query. Tolan v. Cotton, 572 U.S. 650, 656–57 (2014) [[AUTH:TOLAN:P1]]. Discretion is lawful, but the way it is exercised may be evidence of motive. The district court assumed the permissions occurred. The remaining question is whether the jury could find the manager would not have selected discharge for the documentation defects without the complaint. That inference does not require a finding that Bellford had an identical history.

III. A REAL DOCUMENTATION PROBLEM DOES NOT NECESSARILY EXPLAIN THE SANCTION
The COO accepted the manager's corrected assessment without independently interviewing Lorne or repeating the audit. 2-ER-170. Appellant has never claimed the final decision treated twelve preliminary flags as twelve violations. Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., 530 U.S. 133, 148 (2000) [[AUTH:REEVES:P1]]. Evidence discrediting the stated explanation of the sanction can be probative even when the underlying concern is real. University of Tex. Southwestern Medical Center v. Nassar, 570 U.S. 338, 360 (2013) [[AUTH:NASSAR:P1]]. The ultimate question remains whether the protected opposition made the necessary difference. Appellant does not propose that every imperfect investigation go to trial, or that proof of pretext requires judgment in her favor. The jury might accept the independent explanation; on this record it should be permitted to evaluate both accounts.
