DEFENDANT'S NOTICE OF MOTION AND MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT
Hearing June 11, 2026, 10:00 a.m., before Judge Rosa L. Vellum. Defendant moves for judgment on the sole retaliation claim. Defendant relies on the accompanying record exhibits A through I and the deposition testimony identified in this memorandum. For purposes of the motion, Defendant assumes Plaintiff engaged in protected opposition and that discharge was materially adverse. The issue is whether evidence would permit a reasonable jury to find the complaint was a necessary cause of discharge. Defendant does not seek a finding that every preliminary exception was a violation or that the assignment complaint itself was false. The requested judgment turns on causation, including the contemporaneous evidence of a distinct operational basis and the absence of sufficient evidence that this basis was a cover for retaliation.

MEMORANDUM — I. STANDARD AND QUESTION PRESENTED
The substantive causation requirement is set by University of Tex. Southwestern Medical Center v. Nassar, 570 U.S. 338, 360 (2013) [[AUTH:NASSAR:P1]]. Plaintiff must establish that her protected conduct made the difference to the adverse action, rather than merely identifying an impermissible consideration that may have been present without affecting the result. On summary judgment the Court must not decide genuine material factual disputes against the nonmoving party. Tolan v. Cotton, 572 U.S. 650, 656–57 (2014) [[AUTH:TOLAN:P1]]. Defendant accepts that distinction and does not ask the Court to determine whether Maren or the lead has the more accurate memory. For this motion, assume the oral approval conversations occurred. The documents still show incomplete contemporaneous records. The relevant question is whether evidence permits a reasonable inference that the company would have retained her absent the complaint, not whether a different manager could have imposed a warning.
The procedural standard is Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a) [[AUTH:FRCP56:P1]].

II. THE CONTROL REQUIREMENT WAS KNOWN AND PREEXISTING
The operative policy expressly distinguishes urgent oral permission from the requirement to make a complete note. Exhibit A (POL), pp. 2–4. The company does not equate every blank in an export with lack of authorization. The policy instead requires reconciliation and permits discipline according to the circumstances. Maren acknowledges that she could not authorize her own exceptions and that an urgent approval required a completed note by the end of the shift. Maren Dep. (MDEP), pp. 2, 7–9. The November 2023 coaching note specifically concerned a missing lead name and informed her of the importance of the documentation requirement. Exhibit B (REV), p. 3. Her February assessment remained satisfactory. This is not an inconsistency: a satisfactory overall rating does not erase a documented instruction about a particular procedure, much less excuse later failures to follow it.

III. THE COMMON AUDIT HAD AN INDEPENDENT TRIGGER
On April 2 a customer complained about a load handled by Bellford. The April 4 request directed Quality to examine a common fourteen-day period for six coordinators, before Maren's written complaint to HR. Exhibit C (AUD), pp. 1–2; Holtwell Dep. (SDEP), pp. 3–4. The common file was preserved. That is objective evidence that the operational issue was genuine and did not originate as a response to protected opposition. Defendant acknowledges the separate request for a sixty-day Maren review was made on April 12 after the manager learned of the complaint. The reason identified in the request itself was a concern about recurrence in light of earlier coaching. Exhibit C, p. 3. The fact that the request came later than notice of the complaint does not render the reason false. The relevant facts concerning the coaching and preliminary flags existed already.

IV. THE CORRECTED REPORT, NOT THE RAW COUNT, UNDERLAY THE DECISION
The expanded query used the same filter over a longer period. Exhibit D (LOG), pp. 2–3. Of twelve preliminary entries, Quality resolved eight and left four open. Exhibit C, pp. 4–5. Two involved disputed authorization and two incomplete documentation. Quality did not purport to establish what was said orally. Fenmore Dep. (ADEP), pp. 7–9. The company accepted these limits in the recommendation, the HR worksheet and the decision note. Exhibit I (TERM), pp. 1, 4–6. The record therefore supplies no basis to infer that the employer fabricated twelve violations or fired Maren on that premise. The distinction between a real record defect and the underlying oral permission matters, but it does not eliminate the documented defect itself. The Court should evaluate the actual stated reason rather than a more extreme reason Plaintiff does not show the decision-maker adopted.

V. EVEN IF ORAL APPROVAL OCCURRED, THE RECORD DEFECTS REMAIN
AQ-4182 lacks a complete timely reference. AQ-4311 contains a manual reference with a blank lead field. Exhibit D, pp. 9–10. Maren does not identify a retained completed note for the former, and she does not dispute the missing name in the latter. MDEP, pp. 7–9. The corrected report also identifies two other incomplete entries. A manager could regard repeated documentation failures after earlier coaching as warranting discharge because the company needs to reconstruct the authorization process when an error is alleged. The policy gives management discretion, not an automatic sanction. A court need not conclude that discharge was wise or proportionate. It must determine whether there is evidence that the proffered explanation did not actually motivate the action and that the protected activity made the necessary difference. The contemporaneous records directly address that explanation.

VI. TEMPORAL SEQUENCE DOES NOT ESTABLISH PRETEXT ON THIS RECORD
The Court of Appeals has recognized that timing can be probative but held that timing alone was insufficient on the particular record before it in Kama v. Mayorkas, 107 F.4th 1054 (9th Cir. 2024), slip op. at 13 [[AUTH:KAMA:P1]]. Its reasoning concerning pretext is relevant to a private-sector claim without treating the federal-sector statutory standard as identical. It requires attention to evidence of an independent basis for the action. Here the customer concern, common review, prior coaching and actual open entries are independent evidence, not solely a retrospective justification. Plaintiff cannot erase those facts by observing that her individual review was expanded after the complaint. Defendant does not urge a bright-line rule based on the number of days. Rather, it submits that the timing fits an ongoing operational review, and the other circumstances alleged do not materially undermine the explanation. A jury could disagree with the scope of a discretionary audit without reasonably finding that the protected complaint caused the resulting discharge.

<!-- PAGE 8 -->
VII. COMPARISON DOES NOT ESTABLISH EQUIVALENT CIRCUMSTANCES
The common review was a screening exercise, not a controlled comparison of discipline. It did not contain a denominator of eligible releases, and the longer Maren window is not comparable numerically to the fourteen-day Bellford window. ADEP, pp. 3–4, 10. Defendant does not infer from this absence that Bellford had a flawless earlier record. Rather, the absence means Plaintiff cannot demonstrate an equivalent pattern of unresolved items in the two employees' actual reviewed records. Holtwell testified that the warning addressed a customer load for which the lead had given a specific confirmation and an additional documentation entry, whereas Maren's permission remained disputed and she had documented prior coaching. SDEP, pp. 7, 12, 15. These differences are relevant to the decision, even though both coordinators were governed by the same policy.

<!-- PAGE 9 -->
VIII. THE FINAL DECISION AND THE EVIDENTIARY BURDEN
HR expressly asked for corrected data and comparison information, and the COO approved discharge on the corrected record and prior instruction. TERM, pp. 2–5. The approval is not offered as a categorical defense to any possible improper motive of a recommending manager. It is evidence of a contemporaneous review that did not accept a knowingly inflated count. Plaintiff may use circumstantial evidence to challenge an employer's stated reason; Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., 530 U.S. 133, 148 (2000) [[AUTH:REEVES:P1]], addressing an age-discrimination verdict, recognizes that discrediting a justification can support an inference of discrimination but does not compel it. In this retaliation case the final causation requirement remains the more specific rule of Nassar. Plaintiff has not presented evidence discrediting the corrected documentation basis sufficiently to support that necessary inference. The case is not a referendum on the completeness of HR's investigation.

CONCLUSION AND REQUEST FOR RELIEF
The Court should enter judgment for Aster Quay Logistics, Inc. on the sole retaliation claim. In doing so the Court need not determine the truth of the underlying assignment complaint, declare that all preliminary exceptions were violations, or find that Maren never received oral permission. The relevant question is the sufficiency of the evidence of but-for causation in the existing record. Defendant contends that the undisputed documentation defects, prior coaching and independently triggered review, considered with the actual decision materials, permit no reasonable finding of retaliatory discharge. If the Court determines that a genuine dispute of material fact remains as to the reason for discharge, Defendant recognizes that this motion must be denied rather than resolved through a credibility determination.
