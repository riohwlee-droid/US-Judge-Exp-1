ISSUE PRESENTED
Whether a rational jury could find beyond reasonable doubt that the particular recorded answer was knowingly and willfully false and material under 18 U.S.C. § 1001(a)(2) [[AUTH:STAT:P1]], in light of the question, contemporaneous clarification, records, and evidence of the agency's decision process.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE
The United States prosecuted one interview answer, not the underlying administration of the equipment award. The indictment identified the April 7 response (2-ER-171). The trial began July 13, 2026 (2-ER-48). The defense disputed the government's interpretation, not the physical absence of two units at the cooperative's premises during the verification round. The parties stipulated to the administering department, location of the interview, and authenticity of designated records (2-ER-164). This distinction matters because the criminal charge depends on a representation made in the interview, not whether a more rigorous inspection would have been desirable.

<!-- PAGE 6 -->

The award schedule identified six instruments. A property coordinator physically checked four; two were temporarily with a calibration vendor. The worksheet displayed different verification sources (2-ER-156). The March 13 certification did not state that the signer had personally repeated every physical observation. It identified review of the worksheet and associated records (2-ER-158). The award contemplated evidence appropriate to temporary vendor custody (2-ER-161). Merrick explained the transfer and later receipt (2-ER-62). The defense does not contend that paperwork and direct inspection are interchangeable in every circumstance. Rather, the written certification used the paperwork-based method openly.

On April 2 the specialist sought further explanation of verification and the vendor receipt (2-ER-149). The ensuing correspondence identified the offsite units (2-ER-150). The April 7 interview distinguished the records from the instruments themselves. Asked about personal checking before signature, appellant answered, "I checked all six before I signed." (2-ER-146). The exchange continued immediately with a reference to the sheet and transfer list. When asked about a visit to the laboratory, appellant did not claim such a visit (2-ER-146). The recording is therefore unlike an isolated sentence recalled by a witness after the fact.

The government presented the coordinator, technician, specialist, and records custodian. Appellant and the operations director testified for the defense. Counsel challenged sufficiency at the close of the government's case and again at the close of all evidence (2-ER-86; 2-ER-111). The jury returned its verdict on July 16 (2-ER-40). The renewed motion was filed July 20, within the period specified in Fed. R. Crim. P. 29(c)(1) [[AUTH:R29:P1]] (2-ER-32). The district court denied the motion (1-ER-5). The court imposed sentence September 1. Appellant timely appealed. Only sufficiency of the conviction is before this Court.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT
The immediate exchange places an important limit on what can reasonably be inferred from the first affirmative answer. The government was free to prove a knowingly false assertion of personal inspection, but not to convert poor terminology into a crime by excising the explanation that followed. The physical location of the two instruments is undisputed; the asserted method of checking is the issue. The mental state must attach to that asserted meaning. Finally, materiality concerns the actual monitoring function: a hypothetical tendency to influence cannot be divorced from the particular information already disclosed and the steps the specialist had identified. No actual reliance is required, but there must be an evidentiary basis for capability.

<!-- PAGE 10 -->

STANDARD OF REVIEW
The preserved sufficiency challenge is reviewed de novo, taking the evidence and reasonable inferences favorably to the verdict. Under Musacchio v. United States, 577 U.S. 237, 243 (2016) [[AUTH:MUS:P1]], the question is whether a rational trier could find the statutory elements beyond reasonable doubt. A court does not choose between competing witnesses or require the prosecution to disprove every conceivable alternative. But the favorable-inference rule is not permission to invent a factual proposition absent from the evidence. The elements derive from 18 U.S.C. § 1001(a)(2) [[AUTH:STAT:P1]]. Here, the recording and the documents are agreed authentic. Their relationship to the allegation is the focus of this appeal, even assuming the jury disbelieved appellant's explanation.

<!-- PAGE 11 -->

ARGUMENT
I. The relevant representation must be identified in context.
United States v. Jiang, 476 F.3d 1026, 1029–31 (9th Cir. 2007) [[AUTH:JIANG:P1]] examines the actual question, answer and extrinsic evidence of the accused's understanding. The reversal there does not establish automatic immunity for ambiguous answers; it does require scrutiny of whether the evidence permits the meaning the prosecution attributes to the speaker. Musacchio v. United States, 577 U.S. 237, 243 (2016) [[AUTH:MUS:P1]] supplies the governing sufficiency standard. The prosecution emphasizes the words "instruments themselves," a serious point in its favor. Yet the exchange never terminated at the affirmative answer. The response immediately referred to documents and disclaimed a specific trip to the laboratory (2-ER-146). The jury could reject appellant's explanation at trial; that does not erase the recorded words spoken at the time.

<!-- PAGE 12 -->

The evidence of a shared understanding is weaker than the government suggests. The witness asking the question had already seen the worksheet and knew it separately identified two vendor entries (2-ER-75). He had not been given a certification purporting to record a new physical inspection of every instrument. Under United States v. Camper, 384 F.3d 1073, 1076–77 (9th Cir. 2004) [[AUTH:CAMP:P1]], interpretation is ordinarily for the factfinder, and a plausible alternative does not itself warrant acquittal. But a rational inference still requires a factual bridge to the defendant's understanding. United States v. Jiang, 476 F.3d 1026, 1029–31 (9th Cir. 2007) [[AUTH:JIANG:P1]] is particularly pertinent where the context and extrinsic evidence resist the alleged meaning. Appellant argues that the immediate identification of the records is not merely a later recantation: it is part of the answer sequence itself.

<!-- PAGE 13 -->

II. Knowledge and willfulness cannot be supplied by the disputed meaning alone.
United States v. Charley, 1 F.4th 637, 643–45 (9th Cir. 2021) [[AUTH:CHAR:P1]] recognizes the need for knowledge of unlawfulness in this setting and permits proof by circumstantial inference. Appellant admitted knowing that deliberately false information to the agency was unlawful (2-ER-102). That establishes awareness of the prohibition, not awareness that his particular words were false. The government points to his familiarity with the records and the distinction between inspection and review (2-ER-103). The same familiarity explains why the worksheet and transfer list were mentioned without hesitation. Under 18 U.S.C. § 1001(a)(2) [[AUTH:STAT:P1]], knowledge must accompany the charged false representation. The jury's possible rejection of his later testimony is not affirmative evidence that he used the initial words with a deliberately false meaning. United States v. Jiang, 476 F.3d 1026, 1029–31 (9th Cir. 2007) [[AUTH:JIANG:P1]] illustrates the need to examine this evidentiary step.

<!-- PAGE 14 -->

III. Materiality requires a supported connection to agency action.
United States v. Gaudin, 515 U.S. 506, 522–23 (1995) [[AUTH:GAUD:P1]] makes materiality a jury question, not a matter that can be conclusively decided for the prosecution by the court. United States v. Peterson, 538 F.3d 1064, 1072 (9th Cir. 2008) [[AUTH:PETER:P1]] states the capability standard; actual reliance or actual changed action is unnecessary. The defense does not argue otherwise. The specialist described the relevance of inspection sources to his review (2-ER-73), but also acknowledged that the original submission disclosed vendor custody and that further documentation was already needed (2-ER-75; 2-ER-78). The asserted answer must be evaluated as a communication within that already-defined process, not as if it were the first notice of the two instruments or the only available source of information.

<!-- PAGE 15 -->

On the actual record, the specialist could request records, leave the matter open, or close it. He received the receipt after the interview and recorded that the item remained open (2-ER-74). The government argues that a report of personal inspection could have affected his evaluation of support. That proposition would be powerful if the statement communicated an unqualified assertion of such inspection. The continuing exchange, however, identified the records. Under United States v. Peterson, 538 F.3d 1064, 1072 (9th Cir. 2008) [[AUTH:PETER:P1]], capability is not disproved merely because the agency took the same steps; nor is capability established simply because a question was asked in an official meeting. The prosecution's theory must connect the particular representation in its actual setting to a potential decision. The evidence of general monitoring discretion does not necessarily meet that burden.

<!-- PAGE 16 -->

CONCLUSION
For the foregoing reasons, appellant requests that this Court reverse the judgment of conviction on Count One and remand with instructions to enter a judgment of acquittal. He does not ask the Court to find an actual visit to the laboratory or to disregard the government's strongest contextual evidence. The requested disposition follows from the asserted insufficiency of proof of the charged statement's meaning, the accompanying mental state, and materiality. If the Court concludes a rational jury could find each element, the sole appellate challenge fails. The separate sentence is not independently attacked. Respectfully submitted, Emmett J. Carrow, counsel for appellant.
