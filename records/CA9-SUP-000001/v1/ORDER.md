UNITED STATES DISTRICT COURT
NORTHERN DISTRICT OF CALIFORNIA — SAN FRANCISCO DIVISION
United States v. Avery R. Denslow — SIM-NDCA-25-CR-014 (fictional)
ORDER DENYING RENEWED MOTION FOR JUDGMENT OF ACQUITTAL — AUGUST 4, 2026
The renewed motion for judgment of acquittal is denied. Defendant was found guilty by a jury on July 16, 2026 of the sole count charging a materially false statement to a department of the United States. He renewed his motion on July 20, within the time permitted by Fed. R. Crim. P. 29(c)(1) [[AUTH:R29:P1]]. The government opposed. The Court has considered the full trial record and the parties' briefs. Defendant challenges the sufficiency of the evidence of the charged false representation, knowledge and willfulness, and materiality. He does not ask the Court to revisit the stipulated federal matter or the location of the interview, and he does not assert instructional error in this motion. For the reasons that follow, the Court concludes the evidence was sufficient to submit the disputed elements to the jury.

<!-- PAGE 2 -->
I. APPLICABLE STANDARD
Musacchio v. United States, 577 U.S. 237, 243 (2016) [[AUTH:MUS:P1]]. In reviewing legal sufficiency, the Court views the evidence and reasonable inferences favorably to the prosecution. It does not independently weigh testimony or decide whether it would have found guilt as the factfinder. That deference does not remove the requirement of a reasonable evidentiary basis on every statutory element. 18 U.S.C. § 1001(a)(2) [[AUTH:STAT:P1]] requires a materially false statement made knowingly and willfully in a federal matter. The indictment identifies the April 7 answer. The March certification and other sentences in the interview were admitted as context and evidence of the elements, not alternate charged statements. The Court evaluates the evidence of the charged representation, in context, under the legal standard, and does not make a new finding that any uncharged conduct constituted an offense.

<!-- PAGE 3 -->
II. THE CONTENT OF THE REPRESENTATION
The recording shows that the specialist initially discussed records and then expressly distinguished records from the instruments themselves. He asked whether Defendant personally checked the six instruments before signing. Defendant responded affirmatively. When asked about the two at calibration, Defendant said those were checked too and referred to the worksheet and transfer list. He then said that he did not think he had visited the laboratory that week and had signed on the worksheet. The defense emphasizes that the clarification was part of the same conversation. The Court agrees it is relevant and does not treat the initial sentence in isolation. The jury could nevertheless interpret the initial answer as a representation of personal verification in light of the specific question, and interpret the later statements as qualifications made in response to further inquiry. The defense's alternative interpretation is not excluded as a possible factual assessment; the issue is the rationality of the inference supporting the verdict.

III. THE AUTHORITIES CONCERNING CONTEXT
United States v. Jiang, 476 F.3d 1026, 1029–31 (9th Cir. 2007) [[AUTH:JIANG:P1]] requires attention to the context and extrinsic evidence bearing on the meaning understood by the defendant. The evidence in that case included uncertainty about the words of the question and other circumstances that precluded a sufficient finding on its record. Here the recording establishes the exact question, which expressly distinguished two verification methods. United States v. Camper, 384 F.3d 1073, 1076–77 (9th Cir. 2004) [[AUTH:CAMP:P1]] recognizes that some ambiguity does not categorically preclude conviction where extrinsic evidence permits a finding concerning the defendant's understanding. The Court does not read Camper as allowing a conviction merely because a word can have a false interpretation. The jury had evidence of the prior email, the source column, Defendant's admitted knowledge and the question's wording. The Court concludes that evidence permitted a rational finding that Defendant understood and affirmatively answered the personal-verification question. The Court is not ruling that every use of the word "checked" means physical inspection.

IV. KNOWLEDGE AND WILLFULNESS
Defendant acknowledged that he knew it was unlawful to knowingly give a false answer to the agency. He acknowledged the difference between the observation and the documentary sources, knew the two items were at a vendor during the review, and testified that he had not personally inspected them at the vendor before signing. The specialist's prior email informed him that the discussion would concern his own checking of the items. Under United States v. Charley, 1 F.4th 637, 643–45 (9th Cir. 2021) [[AUTH:CHAR:P1]], the government must prove the relevant knowledge of unlawfulness; it may rely on circumstances from which the jury reasonably infers the mental state. The admission here does not prove knowing falsity by itself. Viewed with the specific question, affirmative answer and prior knowledge, the evidence allowed the jury to infer that Defendant deliberately gave the false representation charged with knowledge of its falsity and unlawfulness. The jury was entitled to evaluate his explanation in light of those circumstances.

<!-- PAGE 6 -->
V. MATERIALITY
United States v. Peterson, 538 F.3d 1064, 1072 (9th Cir. 2008) [[AUTH:PETER:P1]] identifies a standard of capability rather than actual influence. The specialist had authority to evaluate the verification record and determine whether the monitoring question could be closed or required additional support. He explained that the source of verification could affect the assessment and follow-up. The Court accepts that the receipt was sought before the interview, was supplied later, and the item remained open. These facts were available to the jury and bear on the disputed element. The government was not required to establish that a claim of personal checking would substitute for the receipt or automatically close the item. A rational jury could find that the representation as to the signer's own verification was capable of influencing the evaluation of the adequacy and source of verification. The Court rejects the contention that the actual continued inquiry necessarily precludes that finding.

<!-- PAGE 7 -->
VI. DISPOSITION
The Court does not decide the weight of the evidence anew. It recognizes the defense's argument that the entire exchange may reasonably be understood as a documentary review and that the receipt request remained unchanged. On the evidence considered under the governing standard, it concludes the verdict is supported by a rational inference on the disputed elements. The Court submitted materiality to the jury as required by United States v. Gaudin, 515 U.S. 506, 522–23 (1995) [[AUTH:GAUD:P1]], including instructions concerning the actual function and the lack of an actual-influence requirement. The jury returned a unanimous general verdict. The renewed motion is DENIED. The Court will proceed to sentencing on September 1, 2026, subject to further order. This ruling concerns the trial record and does not establish the result of any appeal.
IT IS SO ORDERED.
