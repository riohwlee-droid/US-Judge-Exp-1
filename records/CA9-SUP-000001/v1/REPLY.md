ARGUMENT
The United States accepts that the next part of the recording refers to the worksheet and transfer list. The words "I checked all six before I signed." (2-ER-146) do not include a separate assertion of a trip to the vendor. The question was specific; the answer was not. Under United States v. Camper, 384 F.3d 1073, 1076–77 (9th Cir. 2004) [[AUTH:CAMP:P1]], plausible meanings are ordinarily for the jury, but United States v. Jiang, 476 F.3d 1026, 1029–31 (9th Cir. 2007) [[AUTH:JIANG:P1]] requires attention to the evidence of the defendant's understanding. Appellant's contention is not that ambiguity creates an automatic defense. Under Musacchio v. United States, 577 U.S. 237, 243 (2016) [[AUTH:MUS:P1]], the issue is whether a rational jury could attribute the charged meaning and find its falsity beyond reasonable doubt after considering the contemporaneous qualification.

<!-- PAGE 5 -->

The government identifies evidence that appellant knew the distinction between physical checking and documentary review. That is not the same proposition as knowing his answer asserted the former. United States v. Charley, 1 F.4th 637, 643–45 (9th Cir. 2021) [[AUTH:CHAR:P1]] permits circumstantial proof of knowledge of unlawfulness; appellant's admission on that point is not disputed (2-ER-102). The contested link is whether he intended to communicate a false personal inspection rather than the review he openly described. United States v. Jiang, 476 F.3d 1026, 1029–31 (9th Cir. 2007) [[AUTH:JIANG:P1]] recognizes the significance of context to that inquiry. The defense submits that the government relies on the wording of the question to establish both the meaning of the answer and the speaker's knowledge, despite the immediate explanatory words.

<!-- PAGE 6 -->

Appellant agrees that United States v. Peterson, 538 F.3d 1064, 1072 (9th Cir. 2008) [[AUTH:PETER:P1]] does not require actual influence. The specialist's statement that additional personal checking could matter is evidence the Court must consider. But the actual inquiry began with known vendor custody and a missing receipt (2-ER-75); the subsequent discussion clarified the supporting source. The defense's objection is to treating the alleged statement as unqualified in evaluating capability. The government cannot establish materiality simply by showing that the broader topic was within the agency's functions. The relevant connection must exist between the particular alleged false representation and a possible agency decision. On this record appellant maintains that proof is insufficient.
