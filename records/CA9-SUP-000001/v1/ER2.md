UNITED STATES' OPPOSITION TO RENEWED MOTION FOR JUDGMENT OF ACQUITTAL
The government respectfully requests denial. Defendant preserved his motion and renewed it on July 20. The government agrees that the motion concerns the charged April 7 answer, not a separate offense in the March certification. It also agrees that the temporary location of the two instruments was disclosed and no property loss is alleged. Those concessions are consistent with the government's actual theory. The jury could find that Defendant gave a knowingly false representation concerning his own verification in response to a question that expressly distinguished personal checking of the instruments from reviewing the records. The jury was entitled to consider all surrounding statements and contemporaneous documents, including those favorable to Defendant. The statutory materiality requirement concerns the capacity of the representation to influence the monitoring function, not whether it actually succeeded.

<!-- PAGE 14 -->
I. THE LEGAL INQUIRY DOES NOT REPLACE THE JURY'S FACTFINDING
Musacchio v. United States, 577 U.S. 237, 243 (2016) [[AUTH:MUS:P1]]. On this renewed motion the Court considers the complete trial evidence and reasonable inferences favorably to the prosecution. The motion cannot be granted merely because a different factfinder might have preferred the defendant's explanation. The government acknowledges that the evidence must establish more than speculation and that all statutory elements are required under 18 U.S.C. § 1001(a)(2) [[AUTH:STAT:P1]]. The question is whether the jury's inference had a reasonable basis in the actual evidence. Here the evidence includes the precise interview question and answer, the defendant's prior review and communications, the property transfer records, the defendant's testimony and the specialist's description of the agency function. These materials address both the meaning of the statement and the mental state with which it was made.

<!-- PAGE 15 -->
II. THE ACTUAL QUESTION PROVIDED A REASONABLE BASIS FOR THE JURY'S INTERPRETATION
The recording did not consist simply of a question whether Defendant checked a list. The specialist explicitly separated records from actual instruments and asked whether Defendant personally checked the six instruments themselves before signing. Joint Exhibit 5, p. 4. The jury could understand the affirmative answer as a representation of the conduct asked about. The next question asked whether the two listed at calibration were included, and Defendant again answered affirmatively before referring to the list. The jury could assess whether those responses represented personal verification, followed by qualification when the specialist asked about the laboratory. The government does not ask the Court to disregard the qualification. It submits that the qualification does not make the jury's interpretation of the preceding answer irrational. Defendant acknowledged in his testimony the specificity of the question and the imprecision of his response.

<!-- PAGE 16 -->
III. THE EXTRINSIC EVIDENCE SUPPORTS THE INFERENCE
The award attachment distinguished observation and documentary confirmation. Joint Exhibit 2 disclosed the verification sources, and Defendant acknowledged understanding those sources before signing. The earlier email had expressly said the specialist wanted to know whether Defendant personally checked the items. Defendant admitted receiving that email. The two reference instruments were at the laboratory during the March review, and Defendant testified that he did not personally inspect them there before signing. The government does not contend that the absence of a personal inspection record alone proves that no observation occurred. It relies on the documents, witness testimony and Defendant's own account in conjunction with the actual question. The jury could conclude that Defendant understood the distinct subject of the inquiry and chose to give an affirmative representation he knew was false.

<!-- PAGE 17 -->
IV. THE AUTHORITIES CONCERNING AMBIGUITY REQUIRE ATTENTION TO THE PARTICULAR RECORD
United States v. Jiang, 476 F.3d 1026, 1029–31 (9th Cir. 2007) [[AUTH:JIANG:P1]] reversed a conviction on an evidentiary record involving uncertain recollection of the question and extrinsic circumstances inconsistent with the claimed intentional false statement. Here the exact wording is stipulated and recorded. The question distinguished the two modes of verification and identified the signer. The difference in evidence matters. United States v. Camper, 384 F.3d 1073, 1076–77 (9th Cir. 2004) [[AUTH:CAMP:P1]] permits the finder of fact to decide which plausible meaning was actually understood when the evidence permits that determination. The government does not require a prior stipulation of the defendant's precise understanding in every case. It relies on the direct question, the prior notice of the subject, admitted knowledge of the source distinction and testimony. This is not a prosecution premised on an inherently unknowable question.

<!-- PAGE 18 -->
V. THE JURY COULD FIND THE REQUIRED MENTAL STATE
Defendant acknowledged that he knew deliberately making a false statement to the agency was unlawful. He disputed that he made such a statement. Under United States v. Charley, 1 F.4th 637, 643–45 (9th Cir. 2021) [[AUTH:CHAR:P1]], knowledge of unlawfulness is a required part of willfulness and can be established through reasonable inferences; here there is also an express acknowledgment of the general legal prohibition. The disputed inference concerns knowing falsity. Defendant's role alone would not establish it, but the evidence goes further. He had read the source column, knew the vendor custody and understood the difference between observing a tag and reviewing a list. He had received the specialist's specific inquiry before the meeting. A rational jury could infer from the affirmative answer to the direct question and the subsequent exchange that Defendant knowingly gave the false representation charged. It need not accept his explanation that he was still thinking of the entries.

<!-- PAGE 19 -->
VI. MATERIALITY IS NOT LIMITED TO WHETHER A RECEIPT WAS REQUESTED
United States v. Peterson, 538 F.3d 1064, 1072 (9th Cir. 2008) [[AUTH:PETER:P1]]. The specialist described the actual function: assessing the adequacy and source of property verification and deciding whether the monitoring item could close, required additional documents or should be referred. A representation of additional personal verification could affect that assessment, even where a vendor receipt remained relevant. The evidence that the receipt was requested beforehand and that the item remained open bears on capacity, but the jury could reasonably find that the verification source was itself a matter capable of influencing the specialist's work. The government does not contend that a personal observation automatically replaces a receipt or that the answer actually caused financial loss. Neither is required. The evidence of the specialist's discretion and responsibilities permitted the jury to decide the materiality of this representation.

<!-- PAGE 20 -->
VII. CONCLUSION
The Court submitted every element, including materiality, to the jury consistently with United States v. Gaudin, 515 U.S. 506, 522–23 (1995) [[AUTH:GAUD:P1]]. The jury was instructed to consider the actual words in context and to find knowledge of falsity and unlawfulness. It was not instructed that an inaccurate answer automatically constituted an offense. The government submits that the entire trial record, viewed under the proper sufficiency standard, permitted the verdict. The defense's contrary interpretation was presented fully at trial and remains a possible view of the evidence. The applicable standard is not whether such a view exists, but whether a rational jury could find the charged elements beyond reasonable doubt. The United States requests that the Court deny the renewed motion and proceed to sentencing on the verdict. Dated July 27, 2026.

DEFENDANT'S RENEWED MOTION FOR JUDGMENT OF ACQUITTAL
Following the July 16 verdict, Defendant renews the motion made at the close of the government's evidence and all evidence pursuant to Fed. R. Crim. P. 29(c)(1) [[AUTH:R29:P1]]. The motion is timely. The indictment charges the single April 7 answer, not the March certification or another sentence. Defendant does not seek a new weighing of credibility, a new trial based on the jury instructions, or a determination of the civil award's disposition. The motion asks whether the evidence at trial was legally sufficient for a rational jury to find beyond a reasonable doubt the charged false representation, knowing and willful making of it, and its materiality. The defense accepts the recorded words, the location of the interview, federal administration of the award and the temporary vendor custody stipulated at trial. It disputes the inferential sufficiency of the remaining elements.

<!-- PAGE 22 -->
I. STANDARD OF REVIEW
Musacchio v. United States, 577 U.S. 237, 243 (2016) [[AUTH:MUS:P1]]. The legal sufficiency inquiry respects the jury's role in resolving conflicts, weighing evidence and drawing reasonable inferences, while requiring proof of each statutory element. The Court must view the evidence in the government's favor for this motion. Defendant does not ask the Court to hold that ambiguity by itself forecloses conviction, or to accept the defendant's testimony merely because it provides an alternative account. But the Court must evaluate the representation alleged rather than replacing the spoken answer with different words. 18 U.S.C. § 1001(a)(2) [[AUTH:STAT:P1]] criminalizes a materially false representation made with the required mental state; it does not punish every imprecise answer to a legitimate government question. The particular statement and its actual context define the question of falsity.

<!-- PAGE 23 -->
II. THE QUESTION AND ANSWER MUST BE CONSIDERED TOGETHER
In United States v. Jiang, 476 F.3d 1026, 1029–31 (9th Cir. 2007) [[AUTH:JIANG:P1]], this circuit considered the actual interview context and extrinsic evidence in assessing whether a false representation was proved. The opinion does not establish a categorical defense whenever an interview is ambiguous. Here the full recording is not disputed. It shows an initial discussion about the worksheet and records, the specialist's attempt to separate those matters from observation, the charged answer, and the follow-up identifying the worksheet, transfer list and absence of a remembered laboratory visit. The specificity of the question is an important fact for the government. The answer itself, however, does not say that the defendant visited the vendor or personally inspected a tag there. The immediate elaboration indicates that the word "checked" referred to the verification process involving the records. Defendant submits that the government failed to establish more than an imprecise answer.

<!-- PAGE 24 -->
III. THE RECORDS AND CONTEMPORANEOUS COMMUNICATIONS DO NOT SUPPORT THE NECESSARY INFERENCE
The original worksheet retained the distinction between four visual entries and two transfer entries. Joint Exhibit 2, pp. 1–3. The signed certification described review of the sheet and supporting records. It did not assert a second physical inventory. The April 3 email expressly disclosed the two at calibration and stated that Defendant had reviewed the six entries before signing. Government Exhibit 4, p. 2. These contemporaneous documents are not retrospective trial explanations. They reflect the subject under discussion. No witness testified that Defendant directed the property coordinator to obscure the source column or attempted to prevent the vendor acknowledgment from being supplied. The government properly argues that an accurate document does not preclude a later false statement. The question remains whether the actual recorded words support the particular representation charged.

<!-- PAGE 25 -->
IV. CAMPER DOES NOT PERMIT AN INFERENCE UNGROUNDED IN THE ACTUAL EVIDENCE
United States v. Camper, 384 F.3d 1073, 1076–77 (9th Cir. 2004) [[AUTH:CAMP:P1]] recognizes that a factfinder may determine which plausible interpretation a defendant understood and answered, when the extrinsic evidence supports that finding. The defendant there had made a prior statement employing the terminology of the challenged question and stipulated to his understanding. Here there is evidence of the administrator's knowledge of the distinction between physical observation and review of records, but not an equivalent admission that he understood his own words to convey the physical observation alleged. The immediately preceding question is the strongest contrary evidence; Defendant acknowledges it. He submits that the same recording contains contemporaneous clarification, and the earlier signed document and email consistently identify the documentary review. General knowledge of the difference between the two methods is not identical to knowledge that the answer conveyed a false representation.

<!-- PAGE 26 -->
V. WILLFULNESS IS A SEPARATE REQUIREMENT
United States v. Charley, 1 F.4th 637, 643–45 (9th Cir. 2021) [[AUTH:CHAR:P1]] illustrates that knowledge of unlawfulness may be inferred from proven circumstances, not that the element is presumed. Defendant acknowledged knowing that intentionally giving a false answer to the agency was unlawful. That concession addresses knowledge of law in general. It does not concede that the answer was knowingly false or deliberately made as the false representation charged. The government may rely on the question's wording and prior notice of the subject, but the contemporaneous explanation and earlier documents matter in assessing that inference. Defendant submits that the evidence fails to establish the required mental state beyond a reasonable doubt because the charged answer did not communicate a deliberately false assertion in its full context. This is a sufficiency contention, not an invitation to impose a heightened legal standard for circumstantial evidence.

<!-- PAGE 27 -->
VI. MATERIALITY REQUIRES CAPACITY IN THE ACTUAL CONTEXT
United States v. Peterson, 538 F.3d 1064, 1072 (9th Cir. 2008) [[AUTH:PETER:P1]] explains that actual influence is not required, and the defense does not add such a requirement. The specialist was entitled to ask about the basis of verification and to consider the answers. The question is whether the specific representation alleged, in the circumstances shown, had the capacity to influence the relevant decision or function. The specialist already had the worksheet and had asked for the receipt. He acknowledged that he received the full exchange before acting, and that the receipt would still be requested whether the administrator reported a documentary review or a personal visit. He had no rule that a personal check would substitute for the receipt. Defendant submits that the government has identified a relevant general subject but not a sufficient basis for finding the particular answer material beyond a reasonable doubt.

<!-- PAGE 28 -->
VII. RELIEF
The Court properly submitted materiality to the jury under the principle in United States v. Gaudin, 515 U.S. 506, 522–23 (1995) [[AUTH:GAUD:P1]]. The fact that the jury made the finding does not eliminate the legal sufficiency inquiry. The defense does not challenge the form of the instruction or the Court's neutral answer to the jury note. Defendant seeks judgment of acquittal under Rule 29 because the record is insufficient on the identified elements. The defense recognizes the jury could reject Defendant's account, but contends that rejection cannot supply missing affirmative evidence. The Court should not resolve a civil grant question, make a new credibility finding or determine whether the cooperative's records should have been more complete. The sole request is a judgment of acquittal on Count One. Dated July 20, 2026.
