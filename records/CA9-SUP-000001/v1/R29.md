UNITED STATES DISTRICT COURT
NORTHERN DISTRICT OF CALIFORNIA — SAN FRANCISCO DIVISION
United States v. Avery R. Denslow — SIM-NDCA-25-CR-014 (fictional)
DEFENDANT'S RENEWED MOTION FOR JUDGMENT OF ACQUITTAL
Following the July 16 verdict, Defendant renews the motion made at the close of the government's evidence and all evidence pursuant to Fed. R. Crim. P. 29(c)(1) [[AUTH:R29:P1]]. The motion is timely. The indictment charges the single April 7 answer, not the March certification or another sentence. Defendant does not seek a new weighing of credibility, a new trial based on the jury instructions, or a determination of the civil award's disposition. The motion asks whether the evidence at trial was legally sufficient for a rational jury to find beyond a reasonable doubt the charged false representation, knowing and willful making of it, and its materiality. The defense accepts the recorded words, the location of the interview, federal administration of the award and the temporary vendor custody stipulated at trial. It disputes the inferential sufficiency of the remaining elements.

<!-- PAGE 2 -->
I. STANDARD OF REVIEW
Musacchio v. United States, 577 U.S. 237, 243 (2016) [[AUTH:MUS:P1]]. The legal sufficiency inquiry respects the jury's role in resolving conflicts, weighing evidence and drawing reasonable inferences, while requiring proof of each statutory element. The Court must view the evidence in the government's favor for this motion. Defendant does not ask the Court to hold that ambiguity by itself forecloses conviction, or to accept the defendant's testimony merely because it provides an alternative account. But the Court must evaluate the representation alleged rather than replacing the spoken answer with different words. 18 U.S.C. § 1001(a)(2) [[AUTH:STAT:P1]] criminalizes a materially false representation made with the required mental state; it does not punish every imprecise answer to a legitimate government question. The particular statement and its actual context define the question of falsity.

<!-- PAGE 3 -->
II. THE QUESTION AND ANSWER MUST BE CONSIDERED TOGETHER
In United States v. Jiang, 476 F.3d 1026, 1029–31 (9th Cir. 2007) [[AUTH:JIANG:P1]], this circuit considered the actual interview context and extrinsic evidence in assessing whether a false representation was proved. The opinion does not establish a categorical defense whenever an interview is ambiguous. Here the full recording is not disputed. It shows an initial discussion about the worksheet and records, the specialist's attempt to separate those matters from observation, the charged answer, and the follow-up identifying the worksheet, transfer list and absence of a remembered laboratory visit. The specificity of the question is an important fact for the government. The answer itself, however, does not say that the defendant visited the vendor or personally inspected a tag there. The immediate elaboration indicates that the word "checked" referred to the verification process involving the records. Defendant submits that the government failed to establish more than an imprecise answer.

<!-- PAGE 4 -->
III. THE RECORDS AND CONTEMPORANEOUS COMMUNICATIONS DO NOT SUPPORT THE NECESSARY INFERENCE
The original worksheet retained the distinction between four visual entries and two transfer entries. Joint Exhibit 2, pp. 1–3. The signed certification described review of the sheet and supporting records. It did not assert a second physical inventory. The April 3 email expressly disclosed the two at calibration and stated that Defendant had reviewed the six entries before signing. Government Exhibit 4, p. 2. These contemporaneous documents are not retrospective trial explanations. They reflect the subject under discussion. No witness testified that Defendant directed the property coordinator to obscure the source column or attempted to prevent the vendor acknowledgment from being supplied. The government properly argues that an accurate document does not preclude a later false statement. The question remains whether the actual recorded words support the particular representation charged.

<!-- PAGE 5 -->
IV. CAMPER DOES NOT PERMIT AN INFERENCE UNGROUNDED IN THE ACTUAL EVIDENCE
United States v. Camper, 384 F.3d 1073, 1076–77 (9th Cir. 2004) [[AUTH:CAMP:P1]] recognizes that a factfinder may determine which plausible interpretation a defendant understood and answered, when the extrinsic evidence supports that finding. The defendant there had made a prior statement employing the terminology of the challenged question and stipulated to his understanding. Here there is evidence of the administrator's knowledge of the distinction between physical observation and review of records, but not an equivalent admission that he understood his own words to convey the physical observation alleged. The immediately preceding question is the strongest contrary evidence; Defendant acknowledges it. He submits that the same recording contains contemporaneous clarification, and the earlier signed document and email consistently identify the documentary review. General knowledge of the difference between the two methods is not identical to knowledge that the answer conveyed a false representation.

<!-- PAGE 6 -->
V. WILLFULNESS IS A SEPARATE REQUIREMENT
United States v. Charley, 1 F.4th 637, 643–45 (9th Cir. 2021) [[AUTH:CHAR:P1]] illustrates that knowledge of unlawfulness may be inferred from proven circumstances, not that the element is presumed. Defendant acknowledged knowing that intentionally giving a false answer to the agency was unlawful. That concession addresses knowledge of law in general. It does not concede that the answer was knowingly false or deliberately made as the false representation charged. The government may rely on the question's wording and prior notice of the subject, but the contemporaneous explanation and earlier documents matter in assessing that inference. Defendant submits that the evidence fails to establish the required mental state beyond a reasonable doubt because the charged answer did not communicate a deliberately false assertion in its full context. This is a sufficiency contention, not an invitation to impose a heightened legal standard for circumstantial evidence.

<!-- PAGE 7 -->
VI. MATERIALITY REQUIRES CAPACITY IN THE ACTUAL CONTEXT
United States v. Peterson, 538 F.3d 1064, 1072 (9th Cir. 2008) [[AUTH:PETER:P1]] explains that actual influence is not required, and the defense does not add such a requirement. The specialist was entitled to ask about the basis of verification and to consider the answers. The question is whether the specific representation alleged, in the circumstances shown, had the capacity to influence the relevant decision or function. The specialist already had the worksheet and had asked for the receipt. He acknowledged that he received the full exchange before acting, and that the receipt would still be requested whether the administrator reported a documentary review or a personal visit. He had no rule that a personal check would substitute for the receipt. Defendant submits that the government has identified a relevant general subject but not a sufficient basis for finding the particular answer material beyond a reasonable doubt.

<!-- PAGE 8 -->
VII. RELIEF
The Court properly submitted materiality to the jury under the principle in United States v. Gaudin, 515 U.S. 506, 522–23 (1995) [[AUTH:GAUD:P1]]. The fact that the jury made the finding does not eliminate the legal sufficiency inquiry. The defense does not challenge the form of the instruction or the Court's neutral answer to the jury note. Defendant seeks judgment of acquittal under Rule 29 because the record is insufficient on the identified elements. The defense recognizes the jury could reject Defendant's account, but contends that rejection cannot supply missing affirmative evidence. The Court should not resolve a civil grant question, make a new credibility finding or determine whether the cooperative's records should have been more complete. The sole request is a judgment of acquittal on Count One. Dated July 20, 2026.
