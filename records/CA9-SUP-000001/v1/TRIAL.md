OPENING STATEMENT — UNITED STATES
MS. RENWICK: The evidence will show that a federal specialist was trying to establish how six pieces of federally funded property had been verified. Four were at the cooperative and two were at a service laboratory. The property worksheet made that distinction. The defendant signed the certification and later was asked specifically about his own checking of the instruments. You will hear the words for yourselves. He answered, "I checked all six before I signed." The government will ask you to consider the question that preceded those words, the follow-up, and the records he had reviewed. We are not accusing him of stealing an instrument or of claiming payment for an instrument that did not exist. The question is whether he knowingly made a material false representation about personal verification. You will hear why that question mattered to the specialist's monitoring work. The government bears the burden throughout.

<!-- PAGE 3 -->
OPENING STATEMENT — DEFENSE
MR. CARROW: We agree that the six instruments existed, that the two went out for service, and that the words are on the recording. That is not the end of the inquiry. Listen to all the words. You will see a certification that describes review of a worksheet, not a declaration that its signer looked at every serial tag. The conversation was about verification, including verification from records. When asked a specific follow-up about the laboratory, Mr. Denslow said he did not think he went there that week. The government asks you to assign a meaning to the earlier answer and then infer that he knowingly gave that meaning. A careless or imprecise answer is not necessarily a knowingly and willfully false one. You should not infer guilt from the fact that the agency wanted better documentation. The evidence, not assumptions about what the administrator should have done, will determine your verdict.
