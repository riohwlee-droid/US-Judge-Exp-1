JURISDICTION AND ISSUE
The United States agrees that the final judgment and timely notice establish jurisdiction (1-ER-1; 2-ER-173). The sole question is whether the trial evidence permitted a rational jury to find that the April 7 answer constituted a materially false statement made knowingly and willfully under 18 U.S.C. § 1001(a)(2) [[AUTH:STAT:P1]]. The government does not seek affirmance on an uncharged statement in a certification, on an omission in the grant records, or on a theory that a criminal defendant must prove good faith. It accepts the burden of establishing each statutory element through the evidence presented to the jury.

<!-- PAGE 5 -->

STATEMENT
The verification records listed six instruments. Four were observed onsite by the coordinator; two were recorded offsite (2-ER-156). The award permitted relevant supporting documentation for instruments outside the premises (2-ER-161). Thus the government did not have to show that temporary calibration was improper. The coordinator explained the distinct verification sources (2-ER-53). The transfer was recorded by the technician (2-ER-62). The distinction between reviewing those records and personally checking the units mattered precisely because the specialist was examining the adequacy and source of the supporting information.

<!-- PAGE 6 -->

The specialist requested the verification source and vendor receipt on April 2 (2-ER-149). The reply and follow-up put the administrator on notice of the distinction the agency wished to clarify (2-ER-150; 2-ER-151). The specialist testified that he moved from discussion of records to the question of the actual instruments (2-ER-71). This was not a case in which a witness retrospectively reconstructed an uncertain question. The agreed recording preserves the sequence. The jury heard it and could evaluate both the question's specificity and the speaker's response against his admitted understanding of the property records.

<!-- PAGE 7 -->

The specialist asked whether appellant personally checked the six instruments themselves before signing. Appellant responded, "I checked all six before I signed." (2-ER-146). When the specialist followed up about the two at calibration, appellant referred to the sheet and transfer list; when asked about the laboratory he did not affirmatively claim a trip there. The government agrees that this entire sequence is relevant. Its inference is that the initial affirmative answer conveyed a personal check and that the later qualification, elicited by more specific follow-up, revealed the discrepancy. The jury was not required to treat the later explanation as an agreed definition of the first answer.

<!-- PAGE 8 -->

At trial appellant testified about reviewing the file and acknowledged the difference between physical observation and consulting documents (2-ER-98; 2-ER-103). He admitted knowing that deliberately false information to the agency was unlawful (2-ER-102). The defense moved for acquittal before the case was submitted (2-ER-111). The jury convicted (2-ER-40). Appellant renewed his motion within Fed. R. Crim. P. 29(c)(1) [[AUTH:R29:P1]] (2-ER-32); the government opposed and the court denied it (1-ER-5). The court's instructions separately required falsity, knowledge and willfulness, federal jurisdiction, and materiality.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT
The jury had affirmative evidence beyond mere disbelief of appellant's testimony. The question expressly differentiated records and instruments, the respondent was familiar with the difference, and the contemporaneous records demonstrated the limited source of his own verification. The follow-up did not compel the jury to reinterpret the affirmative response as an answer solely about paperwork. The jury could infer knowledge and willfulness from the wording, knowledge of the verification process and awareness of the prohibition on deliberate falsehood. A reported personal check could affect what supporting records the agency regarded as necessary. That the specialist ultimately continued monitoring is relevant context, not an additional element of actual influence.

<!-- PAGE 10 -->

STANDARD OF REVIEW
Under Musacchio v. United States, 577 U.S. 237, 243 (2016) [[AUTH:MUS:P1]], review asks whether any rational trier could find the statutory elements beyond reasonable doubt in light of the evidence and reasonable inferences favorable to the verdict. De novo review does not authorize a new credibility trial. The United States must establish the elements in 18 U.S.C. § 1001(a)(2) [[AUTH:STAT:P1]] and may not shift the burden to appellant. Neither the district court's denial of the motion nor the jury's finding itself is affirmative evidence of the elements. The government relies on the recorded words, the surrounding documents, and testimony about the speaker's knowledge and the specialist's function. Those materials furnish the requisite evidentiary bridge.

ARGUMENT
I. A rational jury could find a false assertion of personal checking.
Under United States v. Camper, 384 F.3d 1073, 1076–77 (9th Cir. 2004) [[AUTH:CAMP:P1]], the factfinder ordinarily determines which plausible interpretation an accused understood and answered. This is not a rule that any ambiguous utterance is sufficient. Here the question itself explicitly separated records from instruments and referred to personal checking before signing (2-ER-146). Appellant had read the certification and knew the source of the two vendor entries (2-ER-103). The affirmative response could therefore reasonably be understood as asserting more than the record review he actually performed. Musacchio v. United States, 577 U.S. 237, 243 (2016) [[AUTH:MUS:P1]] requires the Court to respect that permissible inference even if another reading could also be defended.

<!-- PAGE 12 -->

Appellant's reliance on United States v. Jiang, 476 F.3d 1026, 1029–31 (9th Cir. 2007) [[AUTH:JIANG:P1]] properly emphasizes extrinsic evidence and context. The government does not dispute that principle. It distinguishes the evidentiary circumstances: the recorded question here is specific, and the surrounding answers include an acknowledgment of documentary sources when pressed about the vendor units (2-ER-105). Rather than forcing an innocent interpretation, that evidence could reveal that the earlier general claim overstated what appellant did. The rule in United States v. Camper, 384 F.3d 1073, 1076–77 (9th Cir. 2004) [[AUTH:CAMP:P1]] assigns that assessment to the jury where evidence supports the competing inference. The government need not prove that appellant physically could not have visited the laboratory at any time in his life; the charge concerns his representation about the verification before signature.

<!-- PAGE 13 -->

II. The mental-state proof is affirmative.
Under United States v. Charley, 1 F.4th 637, 643–45 (9th Cir. 2021) [[AUTH:CHAR:P1]], the jury may infer knowledge of unlawful conduct from the proven circumstances. Here appellant expressly acknowledged knowledge of the prohibition (2-ER-102). His testimony also established familiarity with the distinction between observing a unit and reviewing a transfer list (2-ER-103). The correspondence put him on notice that the specialist was asking about the verification source. The jury could combine these facts with the direct wording of the question. This is not strict liability for imperfect terminology. The inference is that appellant deliberately communicated personal checking despite knowing that the relevant verification was based on the records. That is the falsehood and mental state the government contends satisfy 18 U.S.C. § 1001(a)(2) [[AUTH:STAT:P1]].

<!-- PAGE 14 -->

III. The verification source had a potential effect on monitoring decisions.
United States v. Gaudin, 515 U.S. 506, 522–23 (1995) [[AUTH:GAUD:P1]] requires the jury to decide materiality; the jury received that element here. United States v. Peterson, 538 F.3d 1064, 1072 (9th Cir. 2008) [[AUTH:PETER:P1]] explains that the standard is a natural tendency or capability to influence, not actual reliance. The specialist testified that if the signer had personally checked the instruments, that would add a verification source to his review (2-ER-73). The award contemplated identifying appropriate supporting sources, not merely listing six serial numbers. The government is not claiming that a personal inspection would automatically excuse the need for a receipt. It contends that the claimed additional verification could affect the evaluation of adequacy and whether further inquiry was necessary.

<!-- PAGE 15 -->

The fact that the specialist already had the worksheet is not dispositive. It showed the coordinator's observations and vendor entries; it did not establish personal verification by appellant. The specialist expressly pursued that question in the recorded interview (2-ER-71). He could assess the supporting information in light of the answer even if a receipt remained necessary. His later decision to leave the matter open (2-ER-74) does not show that the statement lacked capability at the time of communication. United States v. Peterson, 538 F.3d 1064, 1072 (9th Cir. 2008) [[AUTH:PETER:P1]] does not allow a person to avoid materiality because follow-up exposed the discrepancy before action was taken. The government recognizes that mere relevance to an official topic is insufficient; the specialist's description of his monitoring function supplies the concrete connection here.

<!-- PAGE 16 -->

CONCLUSION
The judgment should be affirmed. Each element was submitted to the jury, and the trial record contains evidence of the representation, its falsity, appellant's understanding of the relevant distinction and prohibition, and the connection between claimed verification and the agency's monitoring function. Appellant's alternative interpretation was presented to the jury and is not frivolous, but the standard of review does not require reversal simply because the defense has a coherent account of the same exchange. No other challenge to the judgment is presented. Respectfully submitted, Lucia M. Renwick, Assistant United States Attorney, fictional counsel for the United States.
