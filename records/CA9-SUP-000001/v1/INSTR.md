UNITED STATES DISTRICT COURT
NORTHERN DISTRICT OF CALIFORNIA — SAN FRANCISCO DIVISION
United States v. Avery R. Denslow — SIM-NDCA-25-CR-014 (fictional)
FINAL WRITTEN JURY INSTRUCTIONS — JULY 16, 2026
1. Presumption of innocence and proof. The indictment states the accusation. It is not evidence. The defendant is presumed innocent. The government alone bears the burden of proving every element of the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt. A reasonable doubt is a doubt based on reason and common sense after careful and impartial consideration of the evidence or lack of evidence. Proof beyond a reasonable doubt is proof that leaves you firmly convinced of guilt. If you have a reasonable doubt regarding any required element, return a verdict of not guilty. The law does not require proof that eliminates every possible doubt. The defendant need not establish innocence. Do not lower the government's burden because of the nature of the underlying award or the duties of the defendant.

<!-- PAGE 2 -->
2. Evidence. Consider admitted testimony, admitted exhibits and stipulations. Counsel's statements and questions are not evidence. The Court's evidentiary rulings do not suggest a view of the merits. Disregard excluded or stricken matter. Direct and circumstantial evidence may both be considered; draw only reasonable inferences from evidence, not speculation. In assessing testimony consider the witness's opportunity to observe, ability to recall, interest, bias, consistency and all other circumstances bearing on reliability. Assess the defendant's testimony under these same principles. The recording and agreed transcript concern the same exchange. The recording governs if a difference is perceived. The stipulations establish the facts agreed upon; they do not establish the disputed elements unless expressly stated. The exhibit source column identifies the source of information, not necessarily a separate observation by every person who reviewed it.

3. Elements of Count One. The government must prove each of the following beyond a reasonable doubt: (a) the defendant made the statement charged in Count One; (b) the statement was false; (c) the statement was material; (d) the defendant made the false statement knowingly and willfully; and (e) the statement was made in a matter within the jurisdiction of a department of the United States. The charged statement is the April 7, 2025 answer identified in the indictment. Do not return a guilty verdict based on a different statement. Evidence concerning the March certification and the remaining exchange may be considered only insofar as it bears on the charged statement and its elements. An allegation that a person failed to keep adequate records, standing alone, is not the crime charged.

<!-- PAGE 4 -->
4. Falsity and context. Evaluate the representation actually made. Consider the language of the question, the response, the surrounding exchange and other relevant circumstances bearing on the defendant's understanding. A statement that is true in its context is not false simply because it could have been expressed more precisely or because the questioner misunderstood it. Where words may reasonably bear different meanings, consider what the evidence shows concerning the meaning understood and conveyed by the defendant. The government bears the burden to prove the charged false representation. You may not infer falsity merely because the answer was imperfectly phrased. Nor is a statement necessarily true simply because another record about the same subject was accurate. Decide the meaning and falsity from the evidence.

<!-- PAGE 5 -->
5. Knowledge and willfulness. The government must show that the defendant deliberately made the false statement, knew it was false, and knew his conduct was unlawful. It is not enough to show negligence, inadvertence, an honest misunderstanding or an objectively inaccurate statement without the required knowledge. The government need not prove knowledge of the precise statute or its number. The defendant's knowledge and intent may be inferred from the proven facts, including the circumstances of the question and answer, experience, prior communications and subsequent explanations, if those inferences are reasonable. The defendant need not prove that his interpretation was innocent. No mental state regarding federal jurisdiction is required, although the jurisdictional element must itself be proved.

<!-- PAGE 6 -->
6. Materiality. The statement must have had a natural tendency or capacity to influence the decision or function of the government body to which it was directed. Apply this requirement to the charged statement and the monitoring function shown by the evidence. Actual influence or success is not required. A financial loss, an award disallowance or closure of a monitoring item is not an element. The circumstances of the inquiry, existing information, follow-up and actual actions may be considered as evidence bearing on capacity. The specialist's testimony describes the function and decisions; you must decide the legal element. 7. Federal matter. Consider the stipulations concerning the award and the Department's administration. The defendant need not know the matter was within federal jurisdiction. The government must prove the statement concerned a matter within the jurisdiction of the Department.

<!-- PAGE 7 -->
8. Deliberations and verdict. Your verdict must be unanimous. Consider the evidence together with all instructions. Discuss the case with each other and reconsider your views when reason warrants, without surrendering an honestly held view simply to reach agreement. Do not decide on sympathy, prejudice or speculation. If a question arises, write to the Court without disclosing your numerical division. The verdict form identifies the sole count; it does not request a special finding on the disputed inferences. If you find all required elements proved beyond a reasonable doubt, mark guilty. Otherwise mark not guilty. Sign the verdict through your foreperson and notify the deputy. These written instructions are the Court's final set as delivered in substance in open court.
